I am not going to pretend a document can substitute for that.

## 2. The pattern, and I want you to sit with this one

Eleven own-product repositories in four months. On 14 September, three are alive: `frontmatter`,
`sgnkai` and `pwa`. Two are dormant for two months: `md` and `knowledge`, both last touched on
**17 July**. The rest have stopped.

Four of those eleven were personal tools that then became a product idea. `md` was your
Obsidian replacement and is now `frontmatter`. `hq` was your ops dashboard and the register says
it has "the multi-tenant SaaS spine built behind feature flags, ready to onboard external
customers". `sgnkai` is your agent setup, made portable so someone else could clone it. The
`skills-registry` was your skills, made syncable.

**Across all four, the number of customers is zero, and the number of users who are not you is
zero.**

That is not a judgement on any one of them. It is the pattern, and the pattern is the thing to
fix. You build a tool for yourself, it works, you love it, and then it becomes a product in the
plan before anybody else has touched it. `md` was benchmarked against Obsidian, Notion, Typora,
Logseq, iA Writer and VS Code before it had a second user.

Two more things from the table.

`md` stopped on 17 July. `frontmatter` started on 13 July. Four days of overlap. You forked your
own working tool to build a product, and the working tool died. The screenshot you sent me
tonight, the one you said is "very helpful for keeping all the contents", is the dormant one.
The thing you use every day and love has had no commit in two months because its successor
absorbed the time.

And `tred`, the one client build in the table, has **772 commits**, which is more than `md`,
`frontmatter`, `sgnkai`, `pwa`, `knowledge` and `agentube` combined. Paid work shipped. Own work
started. That is the 1.21 engineering days a week, seen from the other side.

## 3. The AIOS, honestly

It is the best thing you have built and it is not a product. Both halves are true and I am
going to argue each.

**Why it is the best thing.** Every one of those 82 rules was paid for. I read them. Rule 55 is
the night the backups silently stopped for weeks because of a `set -e` edge case. Rule 61 is
the afternoon a filter deleted 163 of 200 rows and a twenty-minute-old backup saved it. Rule 75
is the push that nearly landed 98 files that were not yours. Rule 80 is 1,997,680 tokens spent
by 16 agents that wrote nothing. Twelve dated seams, each one a session where something broke
and you made sure it could not break the same way twice. The chat guard that has caught me
three times this session is part of it. It is the reason the output you get from an agent is
better than what most people get, and it took five months of scars to build.

**Why it is not a product.** It is 10,033 words tuned to one person's failures. Nobody adopts 82
rules. Nobody installs 31 hooks and 156 scripts on day one. A stranger who cloned `sgnkai`
would spend a week understanding it and then start deleting. The `aios-site` repository, eight
pages generated from live state to explain it, has zero commits since it was made. A workshop
is not a kit.

**And here is the thing that matters.** Your `CLAUDE.md` is 10,033 words. The Claude Code
manual, in its own troubleshooting section, lists "my CLAUDE.md is too large" as a known
failure, next to "not followed" and "lost after compaction". You are not building a solution to
that problem from the outside. You have been living it for five months, and the AIOS is your
answer to it: rules with a reason and a date, so the file can grow without rotting.

That is the one pattern inside the AIOS that is a product. Not the orchestrator, not the
skills, not the hooks. The idea that an instruction file is a living document that needs
reasons, dates, and a way to know whether it was obeyed. You invented that for yourself because
nothing did it, and the evidence in section 5 says a lot of other people need it and have
nothing either.

## 4. What you keep saying you want

You have said it three ways this week, and it is worth writing down exactly: simple software,
downloaded to your machine, you tell it your idea, it produces a kickoff prompt, you take that
to Claude. Later, maybe, the code gets generated inside our own thing.

I have to be straight about this. That product is GitHub's Spec Kit. "Define what to build
before building it, with any AI coding agent." Free, open source, 136,339 stars, shipped 1.0.0
last month, pushed the day before yesterday. AWS built the paid version and called it Kiro. The
future version you described, where code is generated inside our surface, is Cursor, and Kiro
is already fighting Cursor for that window.

So "download it, talk to it, get a prompt" is a free thing from GitHub, and it is not what makes
your prompts good anyway.

**What makes your kickoff prompts good is the 10,033 words behind them.** The rules, the voice,
the design system, the corpus of what went wrong. Spec Kit generates a prompt from nothing. Your
prompts come from five months of context. The product is not the prompt. The product is the
place the context lives, and the reason it stays true.

The instinct is right. The packaging was one layer too shallow.

## 5. What actually sells

I will give you the sentence, then the evidence for each half of it, then the buyer.

> **frontmatter is where you write, review and keep current the files your agents obey.**

**The editor half is a front door, not a business.** Nobody in this market charges for an
editor. Obsidian gives it away and charges for Sync and Publish. The tools a developer reaches
for to do one quick markdown job, dillinger, StackEdit, Excalidraw, all show zero sign-in words
on the landing page and put the editor on screen. And the space is stale: StackEdit has 23,083
stars and has not been pushed since **July 2023**. You already built the editor. `md` has 416
commits, a Tauri build, and a design you actually like. It has been sitting still for two
months. It is the door.

**The product half is the instruction files, and this is where the evidence is.**

- `AGENTS.md` is used by over 60,000 open-source projects and is stewarded by the Agentic AI
  Foundation under the Linux Foundation. Roughly two dozen agents read it. Fetched 13
  September.
- Files nest and the nearest one wins. The main OpenAI repository holds **88 of them**.
- They are maintained, heavily: 77 commits touching the file in `openai/codex`, 64 in
  `apache/airflow`, 35 in `vercel/next.js`. Sampled 13 September.
- They are large: `apache/airflow`'s is 36,577 bytes, roughly 6,100 words.
- They drift: `apache/airflow` and `vercel/next.js` both keep `CLAUDE.md` as a git symlink to
  `AGENTS.md` to avoid two copies, while `LibreChat` kept two and they have drifted to 7,415
  bytes against 23,170.
- The vendor documents the failures: not followed, too large, lost after compaction.
- And yours is 10,033 words. You are the customer.

**What none of the competitors is.** Spec Kit writes the files and leaves you in VS Code. Kiro
is an IDE. Tessl is a platform. Not one of them is the place a person reads what the agent was
told, says "no, not that", and keeps the file small enough that it still gets obeyed.
Generation is one act. This is the thing that never ends, and nobody is standing there.

**The buyer.** Not the individual, who has one file and will not pay. The team, which has the
monorepo problem, 88 files, four people editing them, and no idea which one governs a given
path. And above that the organisation, because Claude Code already documents a managed policy
file that IT deploys across every user, with no tooling around it whatsoever. Same artefact at
three scales. One file, then 88, then a policy. That is how one product sells to a developer
and to an enterprise without being two products.

## 6. What to cut, by name

Not because any of it is bad. Because one person at 1.21 days a week can carry one thing, and
every item below is time taken from that thing.

**Stop treating as products.** `hq`, `knowledge`, `skills-registry`, `rawl`, `agentube`,
`sgnkos`, `aios-site`. They are tools you built for yourself. Keep using the ones you use. Do
not put them in a plan.

**The AIOS as something to ship.** Extract three things from it and park the rest. The
learned-rule format. The gate that checks a file for the things you have banned. The skill file
format, because a skill is a markdown file an agent reads, which is the same artefact. The
orchestrator, the batteries, the 156 scripts stay yours.

**`pwa`.** Fifty-four commits and alive. Park it with a date. It is a second product.

**In frontmatter itself.** The 33-file kit as the headline claim. Review state as the headline
claim. Presentations. Live collaborative editing. Writing to a customer's repository. Every
one of these is argued against in the two documents from this weekend and I am not going to
repeat the arguments here.

**Desktop first.** You want it downloaded, and I understand why: it is the privacy story and
it is how you use `md`. The Tauri build already exists, so this is not a fight. But the web
version is what a stranger meets, and it is the one that can be judged in four weeks. Ship both
from the one tree, lead with the web, and let the desktop be the thing a paying user gets.

## 7. The plan, in phases that can each be judged

Each phase has one thing to ship, one number that says whether it worked, and one thing you
learn even if it does not. That last column is the answer to your question about regret.

### Phase 0. The front door. Four weeks.

Revive `md` as the editor at `/`. No account until the first save. Paste, clean up, export to
PDF and Word, wire the browser print path that is written and unused. Fix the export so the
zero-bytes claim is true. One sample in the empty state.

Ninety percent of this exists. It has been dormant since 17 July.

**Judge it by:** twenty named people outside the studio open it and ten of them export
something. **You learn even if it fails:** whether strangers will touch an editor with no login,
which nobody has ever measured for this product.

### Phase 1. The one new thing. Four weeks.

Point it at a repository. Show every `AGENTS.md`, `CLAUDE.md` and rules file in the tree. For
any path, show which file governs it. Show each file's size against the point where the vendor
says it stops being followed. Show what an agent changed and no person has read. Offer the
learned-rule format as a template: never X, because Y, verified on a date.

This is the thing no one has. It is small. It is the AIOS pattern, extracted.

**Judge it by:** of the twenty, five run coding agents on a team, and three of those say the
which-file-wins view told them something they did not know. **You learn even if it fails:**
whether the drift problem is felt or only visible, which decides whether there is a business
here at all.

### Phase 2. The reasons to pay. Eight weeks.

Share a link and comment on it, not live editing. The kit generator as "start from here", never
as the claim. Storage and sync, your own key, templates. This is the Pro tier and the first
Team feature.

**Judge it by:** one team pays. **You learn even if it fails:** the price question, which
nothing else answers.

### Gates between phases

Twenty hand-made kits before any kit code, which is already the rule. No Phase 1 until Phase 0's
number is in. No Phase 2 until three people on a team have said the Phase 1 view was news to
them. If Phase 1's number does not come in, stop, and it will have cost eight weeks and you will
know something nobody knows today.

## 8. On the anxiety

You said you want to be sure that if it fails you will not feel you did not try. I am going to
answer that directly because I think the fear is pointing at the wrong thing.

The way to guarantee that regret is to build for another five months without a user. Then it
fails and you never find out why, and "we tried our best" is not available to you because the
one thing that was never tried was showing it to someone.

The way to make "we tried" true is small and it is fast. Four weeks. One thing that exists
already. Twenty people whose names you write down first. A number you agree on before you start
that says whether it worked. If the number comes in, you have a product. If it does not, you
have spent four weeks and you know exactly what did not land, and you can say, with the record
to back it, that it was tried properly.

Every own-product in section 1 was built to a high standard and then not shown to anyone. That
is the thing to change. Not the standard. The showing.

## 9. What I do not know, and it has not moved

**Nobody has been asked whether they would pay.** Not once, across five months and roughly 280
repositories. Everything above is inferred from public behaviour, from what other companies
charge, and from your own file. It could be wrong in either direction. If ten people tell you
they want a beautiful online markdown editor with AI and nothing else, section 5 is wrong and
you should build that. If three teams say the which-file-wins view told them something, section
5 is right and it is worth more than the wide version.

**The pace is one person's.** Every date above assumes 1.21 days a week and one author, which
is what git says. If Amit is building, the phases compress and `B1` needs answering first.

**The instruction-file sample is eight repositories, chosen because they are large.** A random
sample would say what a median project does and might say something different.

None of that is resolved by another document. It is resolved by Phase 0 and twenty names.
