# Part one. Five facts that moved while we were planning

## 1.1 Agents now read more than people do

`[M]` Mintlify published its State of Knowledge report on **16 September 2026**, two days ago,
from 329 respondents.

- In August 2026, across their sites, agents made **257 million requests** against **131 million**
  human page loads. Close to two to one.
- **83 per cent** of that agent traffic arrived through machine-friendly routes: markdown versions
  of pages, `llms.txt`, and agent skills.
- Adding a link to `llms.txt` inside the markdown version of a page was a **twentyfold**
  improvement over HTML in their 2,400 controlled tests, and cut token use by up to 60 per cent.
- **7 per cent** of companies have made every surface they own readable by an agent.

**What it means for us.** Our published page and portfolio are described nowhere as serving a
markdown twin or an `llms.txt`. If you publish from frontmatter today, the larger half of your
readers cannot read you properly.

## 1.2 Almost nobody lets an agent publish unreviewed

`[M]` Same report, and this is the number the whole product rests on.

> "83% of respondents say AI agents now draft documentation updates for their team. But humans
> haven't left the loop, only 9% let agents publish without review."

- So **91 per cent insist on a review step**, and the only working review surface in the category
  is a pull request.
- `[M]` Mintlify's own telemetry: **367,000** agent-directed documentation updates between
  February and August 2026, 95 per cent of them fired by webhook or cron, 61 per cent of the
  resulting pull requests merged.

**What it means for us.** Our central bet stops being a belief and becomes a measured preference.
It also tells us who the customer is. A person on a documentation team who does not use GitHub is
reviewing agent output in a pull request, or not at all.

## 1.3 Writing documents is what people use AI for

`[M]` Stack Overflow's developer survey, AI section. Note the date: the 2026 edition is not
published, so these are the **2025** figures and must be dated as such.

Task people mostly use AI for | Share
Documenting code | 30.8%
Creating or maintaining documentation | 24.8%
Debugging or fixing code | 20.7%
Writing code | 16.9%
Committing and reviewing code | 10.2%

And the frustration, in the survey's own words, on 25,332 responses:

> "The biggest single frustration, cited by 66% of developers, is dealing with 'AI solutions that
> are almost right, but not quite'."

On trust, on 12,941 responses: **46 per cent actively distrust** the accuracy of AI tools against
**33 per cent** who trust it. Three per cent report highly trusting it.

**What it means for us.** Documents are the top use, not a side use. And "almost right, but not
quite" is the exact condition the change queue exists for.

# Part two. Somebody is already standing on our ground

Four products landed on or beside our position between June and September 2026. Three are not a
commercial threat. The fourth shipped two days ago and is funded.

## 2.1 Ritemark took our sentence

`[M]` Home page headline, verbatim: **"Markdown editor with AI agents."** Subheadline: "Claude,
Codex, and Gemini work alongside you in a visual editor. They read your files, edit them, create
new ones. You stay in control."

- On files: "No upload, no indexing, no copy-paste. The agent works on the same Markdown files you
  do, right where they already live."
- Closest to our projection law: "Drafts in `~/docs/q2-strategy/` stay in `~/docs/q2-strategy/`."
- On price: "Completely free to download and use. No subscription, no account, no hidden fees."
- macOS and Windows. Open source.

**What they skipped:** no MCP anywhere on their comparison page, no change queue, no accept or
reject, no byte-range splice, no refusal, no web, no phone, no sharing, no collaboration. Their
control story is that the terminal is visible, which is not review.

**What it costs us.** The headline is gone. "A markdown editor with AI agents" is now a commodity
claim and we should stop reaching for it.

## 2.2 OpenMarkdown built the agent bridge, and handed us the design

`[M]` Launched 18 July 2026, 190 upvotes on Product Hunt. 61 stars. Free. macOS and Linux get the
agent bridge, Windows does not yet. Its own line on models: "No AI inside. That's the point. It
runs on the agent you already have."

**Its eight MCP tools**, verbatim: `open_file`, `open_folder`, `reveal`, `get_context`,
`execute_command`, `read_section`, `write_section`, `wait_for_change`.

**How it stops the agent clobbering you**, verbatim: writes are "section-scoped with optimistic
concurrency", and "if you and the agent touch the same section at the same time, the agent's write
comes back as a `CONFLICT` so your unsaved edit is never clobbered".

**Three things to take from it.**

- Our refusal law was arrived at independently by somebody else, and shipped. Good evidence the law
  is right. Also evidence it is not by itself a moat.
- `wait_for_change` is the one idea in the set we have not had. The agent parks and waits for the
  person to edit, instead of finishing and leaving. That turns the editor into the meeting point
  rather than the drop box.
- `reveal` scrolls the person's window to the heading the agent is talking about. Shared attention
  with no collaboration engine behind it.

## 2.3 OpenKnowledge gave us our own marketing line, by accident

`[M]` Posted to Hacker News as a Show HN on 25 June 2026, **381 points and 173 comments**, the highest-scoring markdown
launch of the year. Free, open source, from a Y Combinator company. It syncs with a conflict-free replicated data type, a CRDT, which is the approach we ruled out.

The top comment that matters, verbatim from a stranger who has never heard of us:

> "Nice. the frontmatter question is the one i'd want answered before trusting it: when an agent
> edits a file does it round-trip YAML frontmatter and nested code fences cleanly, or does that
> stuff get mangled? every 'wysiwyg markdown' tool i've tried falls apart there."

The founder's answer, verbatim: "we do optimistic parsing of it, haven't seen any issues with it."

**Read that again.** The single highest-value question asked at the top of our category was
answered with a hope. We have 8,513 byte-pinned files and a corpus gate that exits 1 on one changed
byte. **We can answer it with a test.**

And the phrase is already our name.

## 2.4 Zed Delta went to public beta two days ago, and it is the serious one

`[M]` Published 16 September 2026 by Nathan Sobo: "Today, we're launching the public beta of
Delta, a multiplayer environment for coding with agents and reviewing what they build."

**Their problem statement is ours with one word changed.** "But with agents generating so much
code, the diffs we're asking each other to review have mushroomed." And: "Why should your
teammate's agent have to guess how you got there?"

- **They left git's review surface behind.** "Last week, we crossed a key milestone: we disabled
  pull requests on Delta's own repository."
- **They store the work between commits.** DeltaDB "extends Git's content-based versioning with
  incremental versions based on deltas. It records edits between commits alongside messages from
  humans and agents." The commit stays the checkpoint. The deltas are what happened in between.
- **A review carries the agent's context with it.** "A review guides you through your branch's
  changes with access to the original agent's context."

**Why this matters more than the other three.** Ritemark and OpenMarkdown are single-player
desktop tools. Zed is a funded team, shipping the review-the-agent product, with a storage model
built for it, and they are dogfooding it hard enough to switch off pull requests.

**What it does not touch.** It is for code, in a code editor, for developers. It is not a document
tool, there is no Doc mode, no publishing, no phone, and no non-developer anywhere near it.

**And note what their model says.** Recording the work between commits, with the human and agent
messages attached, is the same instinct as T11. They did it at the storage layer. We would do it
at the document layer. `[O]` Our settled position against conflict-free replicated data types now
has three shipped products on the other side of it, and the rebuttal the plan already owes for Zed
is overdue.

## 2.5 What none of them did

`[M]` The paid incumbents, read from their own pricing pages today: Confluence with Rovo credits,
Dropbox Dash whose four tabs are Find, Answer, Organize and Share with no write tab, Slite at
twenty dollars a seat, Notion where the agent starts at Business, Craft where AI is credits.

**Every one of them shipped retrieval, chat and generation. Not one shipped an accept step on a
machine-written change.**

- The free tools took "agents can edit your markdown".
- The paid tools took "ask your documents a question".
- **Nobody took "approve what the machine wrote."**

`[O]` And note the second hole. Every one of them meters the AI. The customer is billed for the
thing that generates work, and not for the thing that reviews it.

# Tier one. These change the plan

## T1. The MCP server moves out of Later, and it proposes rather than writes

**The demand.**

- `[O]` Obsidian's live plugin registry holds **7,706 plugins**. About **95** of them exist to join
  an agent to a vault. The largest, `realclaudian`, has **2,135,277 downloads**. Six of the top
  seventeen exist for one purpose, to expose a person's notes over MCP: `vault-as-mcp`,
  `mcp-tools`, `semantic-vault-mcp`, `cli-rest-mcp`, `mcp-rest`, and the REST API extension.
- `[M]` `Aider-AI/aider#3314`, titled simply "MCP SUPPORT", has **196 reactions**. A second asking
  the same thing has 137.
- `[M]` Both new rivals in part two built one. It is what got them written about.

**Section 6 of our own plan sets the rule:** a capability that is a top add-on in three or more
ecosystems ships built in. Ninety-five people have shipped this add-on in the one ecosystem we
copy our feature list from. It is in our last column.

**It is technically open to us.** `[M]` The MCP specification of 28 July 2026 defines a
**Streamable HTTP** transport alongside stdio, and says "Protocol semantics are identical on every
transport". Authorization over HTTP is OAuth 2.1, where "A protected MCP server acts as an OAuth
2.1 resource server". So a hosted editor can serve agents. A desktop editor can only serve the
agent on that laptop.

**The shape, and this is the whole point.** `[M]` Every write-capable document server in the
official registry is a `write_file` or an `edit_file`. None has a queue, a proposal or a refusal.

- Documents as **Resources** over `file://` URIs, with subscribe for live updates.
- **Three tools only.** One proposes a splice into the change queue and returns a proposal id. One
  reports the queue. One locates a byte range and says plainly when the range is ambiguous.
- **Scopes map onto our tiers.** `documents:read` costs nothing. `documents:propose` writes to the
  queue. `documents:write` is the one we may never grant.
- **And one more scope, because people are asking for it.** `[M]` Cursor's forum carries a request
  to "lock certain files from my codebase in Cursor AI, preventing them from being accidentally
  modified by the AI". Nothing in the field ships it: permission systems gate the **tool**, not the
  **file**. A per-document lock that says an agent may read this and may not write it is one flag
  in our data model and one check in the propose path.

**Effort.** Medium for read-only over Resources. Large for propose-and-queue, because the
interesting part is the refusal contract, not the transport.

**Verdict. Must-have, and it moves into the build.** Do not ship another `edit_file`.

## T2. The instruction-file set becomes the headline of S11, not a tab in it

**The demand.**

- `[M]` **6,644 reactions** on `anthropics/claude-code#6235`. The loudest artefact in the round.
- `[M]` `agentsmd/agents.md#91` on the duplication, **134 reactions**, listing four different
  global paths for four tools. `#9` on directory support, **109 reactions**, listing seven
  separate per-tool rules folders. `#11` on imports, **70 reactions, every one a thumbs up**.
- `[O]` An ordinary team on Claude Code, Cursor and Copilot keeps **four files** for one set of
  rules. Add Windsurf and Zed and it is six or seven.

**What is shipped today.** A shell command. The agents.md site's own advice is a symlink. Claude's
memory page offers `ln -s AGENTS.md CLAUDE.md` and notes it needs Administrator rights on Windows.

**Nobody ships** a view that shows the whole set at once and says when two copies have drifted
apart.

**What we build.** List every instruction file in the repository. Say which tool reads each. Flag a
file that is a copy rather than a link. Diff two copies that have drifted, which our byte-exact
engine does already.

**And there is already a specification for what to check.** `[M]` A peer-reviewed catalogue,
"Configuration Smells in AGENTS.md Files", measured 100 popular repositories carrying one of these
files. Its own summary box: "We detected at least one smell in 91 agent configuration files. Thus,
only nine files were found to be smell-free."

Its six named defects are a ready-made problems panel: context bloat, skill leakage, lint leakage
(rules a linter already enforces), blind references to files that are not there, and two more.

**The honest caveat, and it changes what the screen may claim.** `[M]` The literature is split on
whether these files help at all.

- **Against.** arXiv:2602.11988, revised 18 September 2026: "we find that providing context files
  does not generally improve task success rates, while increasing inference cost by over 20% on
  average."
- **Against, independently.** arXiv:2607.27250: "Context strategy does not measurably move
  correctness on either agent", across 288 evaluated runs.
- **For, on a different axis.** arXiv:2601.20404 measured "a lower median runtime (28.64%) and
  reduced output token consumption (16.58%)".
- **And Vercel's eval from 1.6** took a pass rate from 53 to 100 per cent.

`[O]` **Where that resolves.** The first paper says it plainly: context files "are useful for
specifying non-standard coding practices", and the repository overview is the part that does not
pay. Vercel's win came from documenting an interface published after the model was trained.

**So the file earns its tokens when it carries something unguessable, and wastes them when it
restates the repository.** We may say the screen helps you write a correct file. **We may not say
it makes your agent smarter.** Two independent studies say it does not.

**Effort.** Medium. Detection and listing is days. The drift diff is a few days more. The six
defect checks are mostly a line count and a git commit count.

**Verdict. Must-have.** Our S11 is a health panel today. This is the screen. And the empty surface
is real: `[M]` twelve linters exist for these files, all command line, and the one editor extension
has **239 installs after seven months**.

## T3. Open a spec folder as one document

**The demand, and this is the cleanest hole in the whole round.**

`[M]` **As of 18 September 2026 there is no product that gives a spec-driven-development
specification a good editing surface outside a code editor.** The three shapes that exist:

- A sidebar in a code editor. The best of them, specs.md, says in its own documentation: "When you
  click a document, story, standard, or artifact, specsmd asks your operating system to open that
  local file." That is the ceiling. It cannot show you the document.
- A terminal. Kiro's review pane, OpenSpec's `openspec view`, specs.md's own terminal dashboard.
- A proprietary desktop app, Kiro's, at twenty to two hundred dollars a person a month.

**And the strongest single signal in that lens.** `[M]` Amazon Web Services decided that reviewing
a spec needs line-anchored comments badly enough to build a full document reader with vim keys
**inside a command line**. From Kiro's own docs: "Press `Ctrl+X` to read the document in place and
stage comments against the lines you want changed. Returning to the checkpoint keeps your comments
staged, and answering the checkpoint question sends them all to the agent as one revision request."

A commercial team shipped a worse version of Google Docs comments in a terminal, because the spec
is a markdown file in a repository and only code tools can reach it.

**And the users of the biggest spec tool have asked for it, in the issue title.** `[M]`
`github/spec-kit#1191`, titled "Spec-Driven Editing Flow: Can't Easily Update or Refine Existing
Specs", carries **115 reactions and 8 comments**. From the body: "Multiple users find it difficult
to update, refine, or iterate on existing specs in Spec Kit without creating new branches and
redundant specification artifacts", and "Workarounds (manual file edit, running `/speckit.clarify`,
copying files) are unclear, error-prone, and not universally documented."

It is a hub issue. It links ten more, opened independently, including "Right way to change Spec
after /implement" and "How to keep specs consistent and up-to-date with spec-kit?".

**The file layouts are settled and public.** `specs/<###-feature>/spec.md` and `plan.md` and
`tasks.md` for Spec Kit. `.kiro/specs/<feature>/requirements.md`, `design.md`, `tasks.md`.
`openspec/changes/<id>/proposal.md`. None of them invented a container format. They are all folders
of loose markdown.

**What we build.** Recognise the folder by its path. Stitch the files of one feature into one
reading surface. Doc mode over the spec. Tasks view over `tasks.md`. Mermaid over `design.md`.
Share by link so a product person can read the requirements without a checkout.

**Effort.** Medium, and most of the parts are already in the plan. The new work is recognising the
folder shape and stitching several files into one surface.

**Verdict. Must-have, and it is the cheapest big win in the round.** We do not own the format, we
do not change a byte, and the files are already on tens of thousands of machines.

## T4. The published page serves a markdown twin and an llms.txt

**The demand.** Part 1.1. Two to one, and 83 per cent of it arriving by markdown route.

**Why it is nearly free for us and awkward for everyone else.** Our source of truth is already the
markdown file. Serving the raw bytes at `page.md` is a route, not a converter. Every rival has to
generate markdown out of blocks. We would be serving the original.

**The caveat, and it is a real one.** `[M]` Ahrefs analysed 137,000 domains and reported that
**28 per cent publish an llms.txt and 97 per cent of those files got zero traffic** in May 2026.

**Both numbers are true, and they are about different populations.** Ahrefs measured the general
web, where nobody is looking for a documents file. Mintlify measured documentation sites, where
agents actually go, and there the markdown route carries 83 per cent of agent traffic. Do not
promise a marketing lift. Promise that a page you publish is readable by the thing reading it.

**Effort.** Small. A content negotiation header, a `.md` route, a generated `llms.txt`, and a link
to it from the markdown output. Days.

**Verdict. Must-have for the published page**, on the readability argument rather than the traffic
one. It is the one place where being file-first is a mechanical advantage rather than a
philosophical one.

## T5. Stamp the accept into the front matter, using Google's field names

**The demand.** `[M]` Google Cloud published the **Open Knowledge Format** on 13 June 2026: "a
vendor-neutral, agent- and human-friendly standard", which "represents knowledge as a directory of
markdown files with YAML frontmatter".

**Then v0.2, on 25 July 2026, added exactly what we cut.** Its five questions, verbatim: "What was
this created from? (provenance)", "How much should I trust it? (trust)", "Is it still true?
(freshness)", "Is it the current version? (lifecycle)", "Was this number produced the way we said
it must be? (attestation)".

The new front matter keys:

Key | What it holds
`generated: { by, at }` | how the content was produced, and when it last meaningfully changed
`verified: [ { by, at } ]` | a list of independent confirmations
`sources` | what it came from
`stale_after` | when to stop trusting it
`status` | moves through draft, stable, deprecated

**Why this matters more than it looks.** We dropped authorship marking on 17 September because
Almanac shipped read receipts and died. That reasoning was about a **product feature**. This is a
**file field**. It survives in the file, it is portable, we do not have to invent it, and we do not
have to defend it.

**What we build.** When a person accepts a change in the queue, write `verified: [{by, at}]` into
the YAML block. That is a two-line splice into the part of a markdown file we already promise not
to mangle. **The change queue becomes the thing that produces the trust signal.**

**Effort.** Small for the stamping. Medium for a real validator wired to the problems panel.

**Verdict. Must-have for the stamping, good-to-have for the validator.**

# Tier two. Good to have, and the evidence is strong

## T8. Suggesting mode, over the queue we already have

**The demand is a build count, not a vote count.** `[O]` Five people independently built a
CriticMarkup suggesting mode for Obsidian between **17 April and 7 July 2026**, none of them
apparently aware of the others, three naming AI in the description and one naming Claude Code.

Plugin | Downloads | What it says it does
`track-changes` | 1,768 | "Review CriticMarkup suggestions in a side panel. Accept, reject, or reply."
`redline` | 834 | comments anchored to paragraphs, in a sibling `.review.md`

**The serious prior attempt tells us why nobody has won.** `[M]` Commentator, by one person, aiming
to "recreate Word's/Google Docs' suggestion and comment modi within Obsidian". Its announcement
thread has **14,535 views and 75 likes**. The repository was created in February 2023 and is
**still a beta plugin**, not in the store, three and a half years on.

**The lesson.** The hard part is not the idea and not the syntax. It is being a product rather than
a plugin. A plugin cannot own the edit path, so it fights the host for every keystroke. **We own
the edit path.** This is the classic case where the thing that is impossible as an extension is
natural as the application.

**One hard line, and it is measured.** `[O]` CriticMarkup's substitution mark collides with GitHub
strikethrough. `{~~quick~>slow~~}` renders in both marked and markdown-it as struck-through text
reading `quick~>slow` wrapped in stray braces. The reader does not see a proposed edit. They see
corruption. Tested against three CommonMark parsers in this session.

**So: speak CriticMarkup, do not store in it.** Hold the proposal outside the file, splice on
accept, offer export for anyone handing the file to those six plugins or to pandiff.

**Effort.** Medium for the core. The queue, the splice engine and the diff exist. The new parts are
the suggestion overlay and the annotation panel.

**Verdict. Must-have.** It is the one place where we can be the first shipped answer to a question
five people asked this year.

## T9. Comments anchored to a byte range, batched into one revision request

**The demand.** T3's Kiro finding. A person needs to say "this line is wrong" on a specific line of
a generated document, and have the agent revise from those comments as one batch.

**Why it is close to native for us.** A comment anchored to a byte range plus a quoted excerpt is
the same addressing our splice engine already does, under the same refuse-when-ambiguous rule.

**Effort.** Medium. The anchoring surviving edits is the real work, and our engine already does it.

**Verdict. Good-to-have, high conviction.**

## T10. Staleness: anchor a paragraph to code, and say when the code moved

**The demand.** `[M]` Fiberplane published "We built a linter for documentation rot" on
**25 March 2026**, opening with: "we've been writing some specs and we noticed they go stale
quickly". On the market gap: "Existing approaches all focus on keeping code examples in sync, not
the prose that describes them." On why not to use a model: "burning tokens every commit to answer
'did this doc go stale?' is expensive for what should be a trivial check."

**How it works, because the mechanism is the finding.** An anchor is a path, an optional symbol and
a git sha, living in the front matter or inline. `drift check` resolves the baseline commit, parses
both versions and compares a normalised syntax-tree fingerprint, so reformatting does not raise a
false alarm.

**And somebody is charging for it.** `[M]` Promptless prices documentation freshness at **$500 a
month** for up to 200 pages, rising to $2,000 to $4,000 for 2,000 to 5,000 pages. It carries a
**second product line for agent instruction files**, priced by instruction count.

**Nobody puts it in an editor.** Drift is a command line tool and a CI gate. A person editing
`docs/auth.md` gets no signal at all that the code under the anchor moved. They find out hours
later when somebody else's build fails.

**Why this is the one that keeps people.** The most valuable thing a problems panel can say about
an agent-written document is **"the code this paragraph describes changed on Tuesday and nobody
came back."** Everything else in that panel is cosmetics next to it.

**Effort.** Medium. Comparing file bytes at the baseline commit catches most of it in days.
Symbol-level anchors need a grammar per language.

**Verdict. Must-have.** It needs no model, no network and no server, and there is a company
charging five hundred dollars a month for the same answer.

## T11. Rewind the document and the agent's context together

**The demand.** `[O]` Measured today across the agent tools' issue trackers, thumbs-up counts:

Reactions | Issue | Title
452 | `openai/codex#9203` | Please make "/undo" back
233 | `openai/codex#2998` | IDE-integrated diff / approval

**947 reactions across four issues in two repositories, all saying one thing.** Let me see what the

**Note what #11626 asks for that we do not do.** Rewinding **the conversation and the file

**Effort.** Medium, and only if we hold the conversation, which today we do not.

**Verdict. Good-to-have.** The strongest external evidence the change queue has ever had, from
people who have never heard of us. Worth one screen, not a phase.

## T12. Shared attention: reveal, and wait for the person

**The demand.** OpenMarkdown built both and got written about for it. Neither needs a
collaboration engine.

- **Reveal.** The agent scrolls your window to the heading it is working on. You read the rendered
  page instead of a terminal diff.
- **Wait for change.** The agent parks and waits for you to edit, then resumes.

**Effort.** Small each, once T1 exists. They are two more tools on the same server.

**Verdict. Good-to-have.** Cheap, and it is the difference between a drop box and a meeting point.

## T13. Review from the phone

**The demand.** `[P]` Follows from 1.2 and T11. The agent finishes at two in the morning. Somebody
has to approve it.

- We already have a phone layout for every screen.
- `[O]` Nobody has built mobile review of an agent's **document** work. The mobile approval pattern
  exists for code.

**Effort.** Small, given the phone layouts are already costed.

**Verdict. Good-to-have, and it is a demo.** It is the single most showable thing in this list.

## T14. A live size readout on an instruction file, and nothing more

**The demand, honestly reported, including the part that cuts against it.** `[M]` The measured
complaint exists: a fresh Claude Code session was measured at 40,613 to 69,534 baseline tokens
across ten sessions. But that issue has **one thumbs up**, against 6,644 on the AGENTS.md request.
And the person who measured it tried trimming the instruction file and reported it "none moved this
baseline more than a few percent".

**So build the honest version.** Show bytes, lines and an estimated token count against the
published limit for that file type. Do not promise it fixes their session.

**Effort.** Small.

**Verdict. Good-to-have, downgraded on the evidence.** Ship the readout because it is cheap and
true. Do not build a marketing claim on it.

## T15. Draft, then promote, as a visible state on the file

**The demand.** `[M]` Three strangers in one Hacker News thread, on a Show HN for an agent-maintained
markdown wiki, independently asked for the same thing: a document an agent wrote should sit in a
draft state until a person promotes it.

**And Google shipped the field for it.** T5's `status` key moves a document through draft, stable
and deprecated.

**Nobody ships it as a state in an editor.** Everyone has drafts. Everyone has published. Nobody
has *an agent wrote this, it is a draft until a person promotes it, and the file says so*.

**Effort.** Small, once T5 exists. It is the same front matter key and one filter on the tree.

**Verdict. Good-to-have, high conviction.** It is the change queue expressed as a property of the
file rather than as a screen, which means it survives outside our app.

## T16. A multi-file change that arrives as a reviewable set

**The demand.** `[M]` Global search and replace across a whole workspace is on Obsidian's
all-time request list and has never shipped. `[R]` Our own section 2 already lists it at 650 hearts
as one of "the three that matter most". People also ask for an atomic changeset across several
documents rather than a version per document.

**Nobody ships** a multi-file edit that arrives as a **reviewable set** you approve hunk by hunk
before anything is written.

**Why it is ours and nobody else's.** Everyone else's replace writes and then offers undo. We have
a queue and a splice engine that refuses when a range is ambiguous. A workspace-wide rename is the
same queue with many more hunks in it.

**Effort.** Medium. The engine and the queue exist. The work is the match preview.

**Verdict. Good-to-have, and strategically the most interesting item in tier two.** It turns our
refusal law from a safety feature into a capability nobody else can copy cheaply.

## T17. A command line, because in 2026 the thing typing the command is an agent

**The demand.** `[M]` Typora's advanced command line interface request is a long-running summary
issue. `[M]` And Obsidian's chief executive publishes the skills that teach an agent to drive
Obsidian's own command line, which 48,515 people have starred.

**Nobody ships** a markdown editor you can invoke from a script and wait on.

**Effort.** Small for open-and-wait. Medium if it grows export and window control.

**Verdict. Good-to-have.** It is the cheap half of T1, and it is the half that works before the
server exists.

# Part four. What we must stop saying

This is the uncomfortable half, and it is the more useful half.

## 4.1 The change queue is not novel, and saying it is invites an easy rebuttal

`[M]` Google Docs already ships per-change accept or reject for AI edits. From Google's own help
page: "To apply changes individually, click Accept suggestion", "To apply all changes, click Accept
all", "To reject all changes, click Reject all."

**The defensible claims are narrower, and all three are about durability and place.**

- Ours applies to a change an **external agent** made to the file **while the editor was closed**,
  not only to what the built-in assistant proposes in the session.
- The decision is recorded in a version record that **outlives the accept**. `[M]` Once a Gemini
  suggestion is accepted, the text is ordinary text and the history credits the human.
- It works over a **markdown file that other tools own**.

`[M]` And note their own bypass. Google's side panel has an "Insert" action that writes generated
text into the document with no review step at all. Two doors, one queue.

## 4.2 We are not first to review markdown prose

`[M]` CodeRabbit has reviewed markdown prose since 2024. Do not claim the category.

## 4.3 The headline is taken

Part 2.1. Ritemark is free and says it better than we would have. Our line has to be about the
accept, not about the agent.

## 4.4 Do not clone iA Writer's annotations

`[M]` iA Writer already built a byte-range-plus-hash authorship model, aimed at AI text, and
published it. Their own refusal rule is ours: when the hash is invalid, "tools should warn users
that annotations may be misplaced".

Their licence note, recorded verbatim because it is load-bearing: "While the format is open, avoid
cloning our work. Draw inspiration from what we made. Change it. Improve it. Design it yourself."

**Read and write their format for interoperability. Do not make it our storage.** Also note their
finding, which saves us the experiment: "The markup always got in the way when editing because
every other word can have a different author."

## 4.5 We retired an idea on a reason its own evidence does not carry

`[M]` Almanac did shut down. Their own farewell page says so: "We've made the decision to shut down
Almanac on January 31, 2025."

**But not for the reason we recorded.** The page gives the reason in order, verbatim. A second
product, Blaze, "has since taken off, growing to tens of thousands of users in a matter of months,
and it's consumed all of our small team's time and energy". Then: "we've decided we don't want to
offer a product in Almanac whose quality we no longer have the capacity to maintain or improve."

- **That is an attention story, not a feature verdict.** The same page says Almanac "has been
  adopted by many of the world's best remote companies".
- Our CLAUDE.md records that per-span read state was dropped on 17 September partly because Almanac
  shipped read receipts and shut down. **The shutdown is real. The causation is not supported.**
- Put alongside T5, where `verified: [{by, at}]` is now a field in a Google specification, the
  idea deserves one more look rather than a closed file.

**One more thing to record.** `[M]` There is a new and unrelated Almanac. `usealmanac.com` is a Y
Combinator S26 company whose home page reads "Meet Almanac, The agent with a second brain", with a
section headed "The wiki that self-updates". Anyone checking our citation will now land on it.

## 4.6 The thing we should say instead

**"The frontmatter question."** A stranger named it at the top of our category, unprompted, and the
category leader could not answer it. We have 8,513 byte-pinned files and a gate that fails on one
changed byte.

**Publish the round-trip result as a public claim and invite comparison.** Effort: days. It is the
cheapest marketing asset available to us and it is already built.

# Part five. What this does to the plan

Nothing here changes what frontmatter is. It changes what ships first.

## 5.1 The seven moves

1. **Move the MCP server out of Later.** It is the most-demanded add-on in the ecosystem we copy
   from, all three new rivals lead with it, and it is the only thing that makes a hosted editor
   better than a desktop one rather than merely different.
2. **Make S11 the instruction-file screen** rather than a health panel on one file. 6,644
   reactions.
3. **Add spec-folder recognition to phase C.** It is mostly parts we are already building, and it
   is the one clean hole in the market.
4. **Add the markdown twin and llms.txt to phase D.** Days of work, measured twentyfold effect.
5. **Stamp the accept into the front matter**, using Google's field names. A two-line splice that
   turns the change queue into a portable trust signal.
6. **Widen the problems panel into the bundle.** Eleven tools, four package managers, nine
   configuration files, or open the file.
7. **Round-trip nested YAML properly**, because four of the top fourteen requests on the largest
   markdown forum are about front matter, and front matter is our name.

## 5.2 What it costs

`[O]` My estimate, and it is an estimate: about **five to seven weeks** on top of the 27-week
appetite, if T1 ships read-only first and the propose tools follow. T4 and T5 are days. T6 is
mostly small pieces. T2, T3 and T7 are the real cost.

**Where to take it from.** The Later column is the release valve the plan already names. Kanban,
table-to-chart and the community can wait behind this.

## 5.3 The one question this raises for the founders

The plan sells an editor. These findings describe **the surface between a person and their
agents**, which is a slightly different product with a slightly different buyer.

**That is a K1 question, and it is yours.** I have not moved anything in the plan. This document is
the proposal.
