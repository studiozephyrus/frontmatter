-- IMPLICATIONS --
  * Almanac is the single most important precedent frontmatter has, and it is a corpus-wide blind spot. It raised $34M from Tiger, shipped read receipts, version-control mode, doc history diffing and scheduled staleness reviews — frontmatter's entire feature sentence — and switched off on 31 January 2025. Every claim frontmatter makes about review state needs to survive the question 'Almanac shipped that and is gone.'
  * The cause of death is the useful part and it is not the obvious one. Almanac was not out-competed by Notion or Confluence; its own team built an AI content generator (Blaze), it grew to tens of thousands of users in months, and they chose it over the docs product. Kortex died the identical way, folding into Eden. The lesson is not 'doc review is a bad market', it is 'doc review loses the internal fight against AI generation inside the same company'. frontmatter's defence is that it is a review tool FOR AI output rather than a competitor to it — that framing is the thing the Almanac story argues for.
  * Bike is a shipped, live counter-example to the assumption that plain text can carry stable addressing. Its own docs state that .txt cannot hold item ids and that row links break on close-and-reopen. That is exactly frontmatter's SAFE_KEY addressability problem, in production, admitted publicly by a developer who chose an HTML-flavoured native format precisely to avoid it. Bike's answer — an open format that is still readable in a browser — is the closest thing in the wild to the sidecar bet, and it argues that the sidecar must carry the ids, never the markdown body.
  * Cambria settles the sidecar version-drift question rather than leaving it open. Ink & Switch's finding is that consistency, conservation and predictability cannot all hold when two schema versions meet, and that lenses maximise compatibility without guaranteeing it. A splice engine that already REFUSES rather than guess is philosophically aligned with that result: the honest sidecar policy is to detect a schema it cannot faithfully translate and refuse, not to lens-translate and silently lose a field. Cambria itself is dead as software (20 npm downloads last month), so this is a citation to borrow, not a dependency to adopt.
  * Draftable is the pricing proof frontmatter has been missing. Prose diffing alone, with no editor and no collaboration layer, sells at $129/user/year and $261/user/year into 1,300 law firms — and it is still Windows-only, with Mac support unshipped. Diff quality is a product, professionals pay two-to-three orders of magnitude above notes-app pricing for it, and the buyer is a firm, not an individual. This is a far stronger anchor than the $4–5 team tier currently in the plan.
  * The git-backed editing substrate has a proven graveyard and an empty review shelf. Forestry.io was discontinued and folded into TinaCMS, which narrowed to GitHub-only; the live successor, Pages CMS, is free with no paid tier and lists inline Comments as an unbuilt 'Soon' feature. So the exact substrate frontmatter sits on has (a) already killed one venture-backed company, (b) commoditised the editing layer to $0, and (c) never shipped review. That combination says the editor is not the product and cannot be monetised — the review state is.
  * The corpus's 138-product list has a real hole at the top, not just the tail. Mark Text carries 61,189 stars, more than anything else swept here, and is actively maintained again with a v0.20.0 release candidate in July 2026. Any competitive claim of the form 'no free local markdown editor does X' has to be checked against Mark Text before it ships.
  * Most of the rest of the 34 confirm the thesis by absence rather than presence. Athens (YC W21, 6,302 stars), Boostnote (16,891 stars, archived twice), Laverna (9,176 stars), Abricotine, nvALT and Quiver are all dead; Byword and Taio are abandoned in the App Store; the survivors — Supernotes at 122 ratings, MWeb at 153, 1Writer at 391, Amplenote at 485 — are tiny. Not one of the 34 ships review state, provenance, or unread-tracking over a working tree. The competitive gap frontmatter claims is real, and the only company that ever closed it is out of business.

-- DECISIONS THIS FORCES --
  Q[Market & competition] Do we name Almanac in the pitch as the company that tried this and died, or stay quiet about it?
     why: Almanac raised $34M and shipped read receipts, version-control mode, doc-history diffing and staleness reviews before switching off on 31 January 2025. Any investor or founder who knows the space will surface it, and the corpus currently has no answer prepared. Naming it first lets us control the reading; staying quiet means the first time it comes up, it comes up as an objection.
     options: Lead with it — open the competitive section with Almanac's shutdown and the AI-pivot cause of death | Include it in a written objections annex but keep it out of the main narrative | Do not mention it; answer only if asked | Reframe the whole positioning around 'the review tool that survives because it is downstream of AI, not competing with it'
     rec: Lead with it. The cause of death is favourable to us — Almanac lost internally to an AI generation product, which is precisely the wave frontmatter is positioned to review rather than fight. A precedent we explain is an asset; a precedent someone else raises is a wound.
  Q[Engine & technical] Does the sidecar carry row/range identity, given that Bike ships a format that admits plain text cannot?
     why: Bike's own documentation states that .txt files have nowhere to store item ids and that links into rows break on close-and-reopen. frontmatter's splice engine locates byte ranges in files that other tools and agents rewrite freely. If identity lives only in byte offsets, every external edit invalidates it; if it lives in the markdown body, we stop being byte-exact.
     options: Sidecar holds all identity; markdown body stays byte-untouched, and identity is re-anchored by content fingerprint on drift | Embed durable ids in the markdown as HTML comments, accepting the body is no longer pristine | Byte offsets only, and refuse whenever the file changed outside frontmatter | Hybrid: offsets as the fast path, content fingerprints as the recovery path, refusal as the floor
     rec: Hybrid, with refusal as the floor. Bike proves the pure-plain-text path loses links in practice; embedding ids in the body contradicts byte-exactness, which is the whole product. Offsets plus fingerprint recovery plus an explicit refusal is the only option consistent with the engine we already have.
  Q[Engine & technical] When the sidecar's own schema changes between versions, do we translate old sidecars forward or refuse to read them?
     why: Cambria's finding is that consistency, conservation and predictability cannot all be satisfied when two schema versions meet — translation always sacrifices one. A sidecar that silently lenses an old review record forward can drop a field and show a document as reviewed when it was not, which is the one failure mode that destroys the product's credibility.
     options: Bidirectional lenses in the Cambria style, translating old sidecars on read | Strict version pinning: refuse to read a sidecar whose schema version we do not recognise, and say so | Additive-only schema — fields may be added, never renamed or removed | Additive-only by default, with an explicit user-invoked migration for anything else
     rec: Additive-only by default with an explicit migration step. It matches the engine's existing refuse-rather-than-guess posture, it sidesteps Cambria's impossibility result instead of trying to beat it, and Cambria itself is dead software at 20 npm downloads a month so adopting its library is not on the table anyway.
  Q[Pricing & tiers] Is frontmatter priced like a notes app or like a review tool?
     why: Draftable sells prose comparison, with no editor and no collaboration layer, at $129/user/year and $261/user/year to 1,300 law firms — while Supernotes sells a whole notes product at £72/year and Pages CMS gives git-backed editing away free. The current plan carries a $4–5 team tier, which sits in the commoditised half of that gap. The evidence says diff and review are priced by professional risk, not by editor convenience.
     options: Hold the $4–5 team tier and win on volume | Two products: a cheap individual editor and a separately priced review tier anchored nearer Draftable | Single professional price in the $100–250/user/year band, positioned as review infrastructure | Free editor, paid review — mirror the Pages CMS commoditisation rather than fight it
     rec: Free or cheap editor, paid review, with the review tier anchored well above $5/seat. The substrate is already free (Pages CMS, MIT, no paid tier), so the editor cannot be the revenue line; Draftable shows the review line supports 20-50x that price when the buyer carries risk.
  Q[Features] Do we ship read receipts — 'nobody has reviewed this' — knowing Almanac shipped exactly that and is gone?
     why: 'What nobody has read' is half of frontmatter's headline claim, and it is verbatim an Almanac feature ('Ensure your team actually reads important knowledge'). Almanac's shutdown does not prove the feature failed, but it is the only real-world datapoint we have on it, and it is not a positive one.
     options: Ship it as a headline claim, unchanged | Ship it scoped to agent-authored changes only — 'no human has reviewed what the agent wrote' — rather than general document readership | Demote it to a secondary feature behind the diff/provenance claim | Cut it until there is direct evidence of pull
     rec: Scope it to agent-authored changes. General read-receipts on documents is the Almanac feature and carries its track record; 'nobody has reviewed what the agent changed in this working tree' is a different, newer, sharper claim that no product in the 34 — or in the covered set — makes.
  Q[Go-to-market & channels] Do we target the git-backed CMS adjacency (Pages CMS, TinaCMS users) as an explicit early beachhead?
     why: Forestry died and folded into TinaCMS; Pages CMS, its live successor, is free, MIT, 3,976 stars, and lists inline Comments as an unbuilt 'Soon'. That is a populated audience sitting on our exact substrate with a review-shaped hole and no incumbent charging for it — but it is also an audience that has been trained to expect this layer for free.
     options: Yes — ship a Pages CMS / Tina-compatible review layer as the wedge | No — the free-tooling norm in that niche makes it unmonetisable; target repo teams with agents instead | Adjacent-but-separate: support the substrate, do not court the audience | Use it purely as distribution — free integration to seed usage, monetise elsewhere
     rec: Use it as distribution, not as the revenue beachhead. The hole is real and the substrate matches exactly, but Forestry's death and Pages CMS's $0 pricing are two independent signals that this audience will not pay for an editing-adjacent layer. Seed there, sell to teams whose agents are writing into repos.

LENS: Review fatigue and the attention economics of reviewing machine output — what the measured literature says about the volume at which review quality collapses, and what that predicts for a UI that marks a large number of spans "unreviewed"
====================================================================================================

-- FINDINGS --
[opened|read-it] Reviewer effectiveness collapses above ~200 lines under review: in the Cisco/SmartBear study no review larger than 250 lines produced more than 37 defects per kLOC, against a 32/kLOC average, and anything below 200 lines produced defect densities "often several times the average".
    ev: "no review larger than 250 lines produced more than 37 defects per 1000 lines of code"
    at: https://static1.smartbear.co/support/media/resources/cc/book/code-review-cisco-case-study.pdf
[opened|read-it] Speed kills detection with a measured cliff: reviewers slower than 400 LOC/hour were above average at finding defects, but above 450 LOC/hour defect density was below average in 87% of cases.
    ev: "when faster than 450 lines/hour the defect density is below average in 87% of the cases"
    at: https://static1.smartbear.co/support/media/resources/cc/book/code-review-cisco-case-study.pdf
[opened|read-it] There is a hard time budget per review session: the Cisco study states reviewers "wear out" after 60 minutes and recommends total review time under 60 minutes, never exceeding 90, because "defect detection rates plummet after that time".
    ev: "Total review time should be less than 60 minutes, not to exceed 90."
    at: https://static1.smartbear.co/support/media/resources/cc/book/code-review-cisco-case-study.pdf
[opened|read-it] Defect discovery runs at roughly 13 defects/hour regardless of how much material you put in front of a reviewer — review throughput is a fixed human rate, not a function of queue size, so a bigger backlog buys nothing.
    ev: "The overall defect rate was 13 defects per hour"; "defect rate is constant across all the reviews"
    at: https://static1.smartbear.co/support/media/resources/cc/book/code-review-cisco-case-study.pdf
[opened|read-it] The Cisco corpus is 2,500 reviews of 3.2M lines by 50 developers over 10 months, ending May 2006 — the study is 20 years old and pre-dates every tool frontmatter competes with, so its rates are the oldest and most-replicated numbers in this lens, not the freshest.
    ev: "With 2500 reviews of 3.2 million lines of code written by 50 developers"
    at: https://static1.smartbear.co/support/media/resources/cc/book/code-review-cisco-case-study.pdf
[opened|read-it] The real per-person review attention budget is about three hours a week: Google measured developers spending an average of 3.2 hours/week (median 2.6) reviewing, across 9 million reviewed changes.
    ev: "developers spend an average of 3.2 (median 2.6 hours a week) reviewing changes"
    at: https://sback.it/publications/icse2018seip.pdf
[opened|read-it] Google's review process converged on tiny units: median change size is 24 lines, over 10% of changes touch a single line, over 35% touch a single file, and the median reviewer count is 1.
    ev: "the median number of lines modified is 24"
    at: https://sback.it/publications/icse2018seip.pdf
[opened|read-it] Review at Microsoft mostly does not find defects: in a card sort of 570 review comments, only 78 (14%) were about defects — fourth of nine categories — and most were "micro" level, while code-improvement comments were 165 (29%).
    ev: "the category defect is the only the fourth most frequent, out of nine items, with 78 (14%) comments"
    at: https://sback.it/publications/icse2013.pdf
[opened|read-it] Google enforces a 10% dismissal ceiling as a governance rule: a static-analysis check whose "not-useful" click rate reaches 10% is put on probation or turned off, and Tricorder's actual aggregate rate ran around 5% (under 4% excluding probationary analyzers).
    ev: "A rate ≥ 10% puts the ana[lyzer on probation]"; "typically at around 5%"
    at: https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/43322.pdf
[opened|read-it] Human reviewers measurably habituate to AI-agent output: across 400 repeat reviewers and 11,429 reviews over seven months, approval rate rose from 30.1% to 36.8% early-to-late (p < 10^-6), and pooled by experience decile from 27.9% to 42.4%, a +14.5pp gap.
    ev: "a population-level shift in approval rate from 30.1% to 36.8%"
    at: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22721
[opened|read-it] That habituation is decay, not calibration: over the same period inline comments per review fell from 1.01 to 0.79 (-22%, p=0.0014) and comment word count from 18.6 to 13.5 (-28%), with a strong negative within-reviewer correlation between approval rise and comment effort (Spearman rho = -0.556, p < 10^-4).
    ev: "inline comment volume decreases (−22%, p = 0.0014)"; "ρ = −0.556"
    at: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22721
[opened|read-it] The same study rules out the benign explanations: human-authored PR approval rates declined over the same window (so it is agent-specific), median PR size was flat (so it is not easier work), and review latency rose 3.5x from a median 3.9h to 13.5h — more queue time, less inspection.
    ev: "review latency increases rather than decreases (+3.5×)"; "median PR size is flat"
    at: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22721
[opened|read-it] Clinical alerting is the mature version of this failure and the numbers are brutal: a scoping review of 34 studies across Asia, Europe, the US and UK found drug-drug-interaction alert override rates ranging from 55% to 98%.
    ev: "The override rate of DDI alerts ranged from 55% to 98%"
    at: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35673040/
[opened|read-it] Forcing a justification for dismissal does not create scrutiny, it creates garbage: in a Chinese hospital study of 14,612 medication alerts, 12,659 (86.6%) were overridden and 88.3% of the free-text "overridden reasons" typed by physicians were meaningless characters or values.
    ev: "88.3% of 'overridden reasons' inputted by the physicians were meaningless characters"

-- IMPLICATIONS --
  * THE HARD WARNING. The evidence supports one, and it is aimed squarely at the headline claim. "18% of this repo is unread agent output" is not a feature, it is a measured 55.6-hour reading debt (800,410 words at 240 wpm) that no reviewer will ever pay down. Against Google's measured 3.2 h/week review budget that is 17.4 weeks of one engineer's entire review time; against Cisco's 60-minute wear-out limit it is 56 separate sessions. A number that large does not create urgency. It creates dismissal, and the dismissal literature is unambiguous about what happens next: DDI alert overrides of 55–98% across 34 studies, 86.6% override in a 14,612-alert hospital study, and 88.3% of the forced justifications typed as meaningless characters. Ship a surface that marks 200 spans unreviewed and you have built a clinical alerting system with the same override curve.
  * Google's 10% rule is the design constraint, and it is a governance rule, not a guideline. Tricorder puts an analyzer on probation or turns it off when its not-useful rate reaches 10%; actual observed rate is ~5%. If frontmatter's unreviewed markers get dismissed more than 10% of the time, the marker is dead as a signal and every subsequent one is noise. This should be an instrumented, shipped metric with a kill threshold, not a hope.
  * The only intervention with measured effect is extreme selectivity, not better presentation. Heidelberg fired 31 distinct rules in 0.19% of 500,274 prescribing sessions and got 57.5% acceptance. That is a 500:1 suppression ratio. Translated: frontmatter should not surface 18% of a repo. It should surface the handful of spans where the agent changed something load-bearing that nobody asked for — measured in single digits per session, not percentages of a corpus. The engine's splice-and-refuse discipline is the right substrate for this: it already knows exactly which byte ranges changed, so it can rank rather than enumerate.
  * Habituation is measured, agent-specific, and fast. Within seven months and a median of 17 reviews, 400 reviewers moved from 30.1% to 36.8% approval while cutting inline comments 22% and word count 28%, with rho = -0.556 between the two. Human PR approval declined over the same window, so this is not general leniency. Any product whose value proposition is "a human will review this" is building on an asset that decays measurably with exposure, and the decay is invisible in the metrics a product team normally watches: throughput looks fine, latency even rose 3.5x, only comment effort fell.
  * The 200-line ceiling and the 60-minute ceiling are the two hardest numbers available and they should shape the unit of work in the UI. Cisco: nothing above 250 lines produced above-average defect density; above 450 LOC/hour, 87% of reviews were below average; detection plummets past 60 minutes. Google converged independently on a 24-line median change and one reviewer. Frontmatter's review unit must be sized to a single sub-60-minute sitting containing a small number of spans — the product decision is what to leave out, and there is no version of this where the correct answer is "all of it, with a badge".
  * Review does not find defects, it builds understanding — which changes what the product is for. Microsoft's card sort put defects at 14% of comments, fourth of nine categories, and named understanding as the reviewers' main challenge. The honest pitch for frontmatter is therefore not "catch what the agent got wrong" (a defect-detection claim the literature does not support at these volumes) but "know what changed and why" — an orientation claim. That is a smaller promise and a more defensible one.
  * Four dead or pivoted precedents, and they died of category, not execution. Codebrag sold "Clear to-review list" with a 30-day trial in mid-2014, was free and open-source by January 2015, archived in 2018 — the paid model failed inside about seven months, and its own best testimonial ("more like checking your mail") names the habituation trap. JetBrains Upsource ended sales February 2022 citing "a gradual industry trend of moving away from standalone code review tools"; its designated successor, SpaceCode, was itself discontinued November 2024. Swimm sold doc-staleness alerts in 2022 and now sells mainframe migration services. Standalone review-state surfaces have a consistent record of not sustaining a company.
  * The demand side is genuinely new and that is the counter-argument worth taking seriously. Codebrag and Upsource died in a world of 932,791-agent-PR volume not existing; that dataset now does. Godot maintainers are on record calling AI slop PRs "draining and demoralizing"; METR measured a 19% slowdown against a predicted 24% speedup. The pain is real and freshly created. But the dead-product record says the pain being real is not sufficient — the tools that presented that pain as an inbox all died, and the one thing that has ever moved override rates is ruthless suppression.
  * Do not build a justify-your-dismissal flow. It is the intuitive answer and it is empirically refuted: 88.3% of physicians' free-text override reasons were meaningless characters. Requiring a reason converts a dismissal into a dismissal plus a lie, and the lie pollutes any audit trail built on top of it. Prefer a design where dismissal is cheap and silent, and where the burden falls on the system to have been selective enough to deserve attention in the first place.
  * Skepticism cannot be trained into the user. A dedicated AI-literacy intervention failed to reduce over-reliance (52.1% adoption of incorrect suggestions) and made people reject correct suggestions more. Onboarding copy, tooltips and "review carefully" nudges are not a mitigation. The mitigation is volume control in the engine.

-- DECISIONS THIS FORCES --
  Q[Design, UI & attention] Should the product ever show a percentage-of-repo unreviewed figure at all?
     why: The 18% headline is the current pitch, but it is a 55.6-hour reading debt on this repo's own measured 4,446,724 words. Clinical alerting shows that unpayable backlogs produce 55–98% dismissal, not action. The number is memorable in a demo and corrosive in daily use — those pull in opposite directions and the founder has to pick one.
     options: Keep the percentage as the headline metric everywhere | Keep it in marketing only; never render it in the working UI | Replace it with an absolute count capped at a small ceiling (e.g. 'the 7 changes worth your attention') | Show it once at onboarding as a one-time framing, then never again
     rec: Keep it in marketing, kill it in the product. Inside the app, show a hard-capped absolute count of ranked spans. The percentage is a fine way to explain why the tool exists and a terrible way to run a session — a reviewer who sees 18% learns that the number never goes down, which is the exact conditioning that produced the 88.3%-garbage-justification result.
  Q[Design, UI & attention] What is the maximum number of unreviewed spans the UI is allowed to surface in one sitting?
     why: Cisco puts the effectiveness ceiling at ~200 lines and the wear-out limit at 60 minutes; Google converged on a 24-line median change with one reviewer; Heidelberg got 57.5% acceptance by firing on 0.19% of sessions. Every measured source says the winning move is suppression, and suppression means a number has to be chosen and defended.
     options: No cap — show everything, let the user scroll | Cap per session at a fixed small number (5–10 spans) | Cap by estimated review time (fill a 30–45 minute session, then stop) | Cap by a confidence threshold on the ranker, however many that yields
     rec: Cap by estimated review time, targeting a 30–45 minute session with a hard stop, and make the stop visible ('that's the session — the rest can wait'). Time is the constraint the literature actually measures (60-minute wear-out, 3.2 h/week budget), and a time cap degrades gracefully as documents vary in length in a way a fixed span count does not.
  Q[Flow & interaction] Should marking something reviewed require a reason, a comment, or any justification?
     why: It is the obvious way to make review feel real and to build an audit trail. It is also directly refuted: 88.3% of forced free-text override reasons in a 14,612-alert study were meaningless characters, which means the audit trail would be mostly fabricated.
     options: Require a typed reason on dismissal | Require a reason only on bulk dismissal, not single | Offer an optional note, never required | No justification at any point; dismissal is one keystroke
     rec: No justification, one keystroke, with an optional note. Cheap dismissal keeps the dismissal-rate metric honest, which is the only instrument that tells you whether the ranker is working. A forced reason destroys that instrument and buys a log full of 'x'.
  Q[Engine & technical] Do we instrument a dismissal-rate kill threshold and act on it, the way Google's Tricorder does?
     why: Tricorder puts an analyzer on probation at a 10% not-useful rate and runs at ~5%. Without an equivalent, frontmatter has no way to detect that its markers have become noise — and the habituation data shows the decay is invisible in throughput and latency, only appearing in effort metrics nobody watches by default.
     options: No instrumentation; judge by user feedback | Log dismissal rate as an internal metric only | Log it and set a 10% threshold that disables a marker category automatically | Log it, threshold it, and show the user their own dismissal rate
     rec: Instrument it and enforce a 10% threshold per marker category, auto-disabling a category that crosses it. Borrowing Google's exact number is defensible because it is a published operational threshold from a system at scale, not a guess — and shipping a kill switch is the strongest possible statement that the product is on the reviewer's side rather than competing for their attention.
  Q[Product & definition] Is the claim 'catch what the agent got wrong' or 'know what changed and why'?
     why: Defect-catching is the claim buyers want to hear and the one the literature least supports: Microsoft found defects were 14% of review comments and fourth of nine categories, and reviewers named understanding as their real challenge. Overclaiming detection at these volumes is a promise the attention data says will be broken.
     options: Lead with defect detection ('catch what the agent got wrong') | Lead with orientation ('know what changed and why') | Lead with accountability ('nobody has read this') | Lead with the engine ('byte-exact, refuses rather than guesses')
     rec: Lead with orientation. It is the outcome review actually produces per the Microsoft data, it survives contact with the habituation curve (understanding degrades more gracefully than vigilance), and it is the claim frontmatter's splice-only engine can genuinely underwrite because it knows exactly which bytes moved.
  Q[Market & competition] Given that four precedent products in this exact shape died or pivoted, what is the specific reason this one does not?
     why: Codebrag went free within seven months of paid launch and unmaintained by 2018; Upsource ended sales citing an industry move away from standalone review tools; its successor SpaceCode was discontinued in 2024; Swimm's doc-staleness product became a migration services business. Four data points is a pattern, and the pattern is that this category does not sustain a company on its own.
     options: The demand side is new — 932,791 agent PRs did not exist when they died | Ours is not standalone; it is an editor first and a review surface second | Ours is a paid single-user tool, not a team-seat product with the same sales motion | Accept the pattern: build it as a feature inside something else, or as open source with a services model
     rec: Take option 2 seriously and stress-test option 4. Upsource's stated cause of death — standalone review tools losing to integrated ones — is the single most direct threat to frontmatter's positioning, and 'it's an editor that also shows review state' is a real answer where 'it's a review dashboard' is not. But the Swimm arc (alerts product to services business) should be planned for, not discovered.
  Q[Research & evidence] Do we test the habituation curve on our own users before we build the review surface, or after?
     why: The Habituation at the Gate result — approval up 14.5pp across experience deciles, inline comments down 22%, rho = -0.556 — is a within-reviewer effect over seven months. If it reproduces in frontmatter, the product's core value evaporates on a timescale longer than any pilot will run, and it will do so silently while throughput metrics stay green.
     options: Ship first, measure dismissal-rate drift after | Build the effort-metric instrumentation into v1 (spans opened, time-on-span, notes written) before the surface ships | Run a small longitudinal pilot specifically measuring per-user dismissal drift over 8+ weeks | Treat it as unfalsifiable at pilot scale and design defensively (aggressive suppression) instead
     rec: Build the instrumentation into v1 and design defensively. The effect is real enough to design against and slow enough that no pilot will catch it; the cheap move is to log time-on-span and dismissal-per-session from day one so the curve is visible when it appears, while sizing sessions conservatively in the meantime.
  Q[Design, UI & attention] Should the tool ever nudge, notify, or badge outside an explicit review session?
     why: A persistent unread badge is the default pattern and it is the one with the worst evidence behind it. Every measured system that surfaces persistent counts of things-you-should-attend-to converges on high override rates; the one that achieved acceptance fired on 0.19% of sessions.
     options: Persistent badge with unread count | Badge only above a severity threshold | No badge; review state visible only when the user opens the review view | Digest at a chosen cadence (e.g. one summary per day)
     rec: No badge. Review state is visible when the user asks for it, and the tool's job is to make that session worth having. A persistent count is the single design element most likely to produce the dismissal curve, and removing it costs nothing that the evidence says works.

-- COULD NOT VERIFY --
  ? The '18% unread agent output' figure itself. I did not open any source that produces it, and I did not find the methodology behind it. I measured this repo's total markdown volume (4,446,724 words) and applied 18% arithmetically — that arithmetic is sound, but the 18% input is an assumption carried in from the pitch, not a number I verified. If it means 18% of files, 18% of bytes, or 18% of changed spans, the reading-debt figure changes substantially.
  ? Whether the Cisco/SmartBear numbers hold for prose review at all. Every rate in that study is defects-per-kLOC of source code. There is no equivalent published rate for reviewing prose diffs, and I found none. Mapping '200 lines' onto markdown spans is an analogy I am making, not a measured equivalence, and it is the weakest link in the attention arithmetic.
  ? The specific cause of Codebrag's commercial failure. I established the timeline from three Wayback snapshots and the GitHub repo state, but SoftwareMill published no post-mortem I could find. Whether it failed on distribution, pricing, the review model itself, or simply lost internal sponsorship is not established — I am inferring the model's role from the timeline and the testimonial framing, and that inference is not proven.
  ? Whether the Habituation at the Gate result replicates. It is a single five-page workshop paper (KDD 2026 SE 3.0), n=400 reviewers, seven months, on one dataset (AIDev). It is the most directly on-point finding in this lens and it has the thinnest evidentiary base of anything I cite.   ? Any published research on humans reviewing AI-generated *documentation* or long-form prose specifically.   ? Whether Google's 10% not-useful threshold is still the operative policy. The Tricorder paper is ICSE 2015. I found no more recent published statement of the threshold and did not verify it still holds.

-- IMPLICATIONS --
  * The headline claim needs rewriting. "Shows you what an agent changed in a working tree with no PR" is now shipped, free, and default-on in Claude Code (2026-09-03), Google Antigravity (2026-08-24), OpenAI Codex, and VS Code. Four platforms, all within the last six weeks. This is no longer a differentiator; it is table stakes, and any deck that leads with it will be refuted by the audience opening their own editor.
  * The per-file "reviewed" marker — the half of the pitch that was genuinely distinctive — shipped in VS Code 1.128 on 2026-07-08, including the hard part: the reviewed state clears when the file changes again. Frontmatter's "know what nobody has reviewed" is now a feature of a free editor Microsoft ships to tens of millions of people. It must either be dropped as a headline or re-pitched at a level VS Code's version does not reach (cross-session, cross-machine, per-span rather than per-file, durable in the repo rather than in editor workspace state).
  * The markdown-specific review surface also shipped: VS Code 1.130 on 2026-07-22 gave agent-changed Markdown files a hybrid editor with Locked mode and range-based feedback comments, on by default. The corpus's assumption that markdown docs are the neglected surface is now two months out of date inside the single most-used editor.
  * Zed is the real competitor, not Obsidian or Cursor. Delta (private beta 2026-08-12) is a purpose-built application whose entire thesis — "software is made between commits", review anchored to spans that survive edits, comments that do not go stale — is frontmatter's thesis, executed by a Sequoia-backed team with a decade of CRDT and text-editing engineering behind it and 679 HN points on the launch. Any positioning doc that does not name Zed Delta is incomplete.
  * Zed's public bet on CRDTs for exactly this problem sits directly against the corpus's settled position that sync is git-merge plus splice journal and never a CRDT. That position may still be right for prose — Zed is solving it for code — but it can no longer be stated as settled without addressing why a team that has worked on CRDTs for ten years reached the opposite conclusion.
  * The remaining genuine gap is capability (c): marking which text in a document an agent wrote. No platform shipped it. The only implementation found anywhere is a VS Code extension with 88 installs. If frontmatter needs one defensible headline, this is the only one of the four that is still open — and it is also the one the corpus already demoted after the iA Writer 7 finding, so that demotion should be revisited.
  * The category is commoditizing from below as fast as from above. 501 GitHub repos matching agent-diff-review created since 2026-05-01, and one free markdown review skill (human-review) went from zero to 1,241 stars in six weeks. Pricing and positioning must assume a competent free alternative exists for every individual-developer use case; the paid case has to rest on something a weekend OSS skill cannot copy.
  * There is one live, dated, six-month-old piece of evidence that the pain is real and unmet in at least one place: claude-code#33932, still open on 2026-09-08 with 266 reactions, where users say the missing diff review is what stops them switching from Copilot. But note what that proves — the demand is for a free IDE feature from Anthropic, not for a paid product. It is evidence of a gap, not of willingness to pay.
  * Timing is now the dominant risk variable rather than a background one. Three of the four capabilities moved between 2026-07-03 and 2026-09-03. Any plan with a build horizon longer than a quarter should assume the fourth (attribution marking) also gets absorbed, and should be scoped so that absorption does not zero the product.

  Q[Product & definition] Do we keep "see what the agent changed that you did not ask for" as the headline, now that four platforms ship it free?
     why: Claude Code shipped an auto-opening working-tree diff panel on 2026-09-03, Antigravity on 2026-08-24, and Codex and VS Code already had one. The headline as written describes something the audience can get for free in the editor they already have open, and saying it out loud in a meeting invites immediate refutation.
     options: Keep the headline and differentiate on markdown-specific depth | Move the headline to the reviewed/unreviewed state and treat the diff as supporting | Move the headline to authorship attribution, the one capability nobody shipped | Drop review state as the headline entirely and lead with the splice engine's refusal behaviour
     rec: Move off it. The diff view is now a commodity and should appear in the product as a necessary feature, not as the reason to buy. Of the remaining options, authorship attribution is the only one no platform has shipped, but it was already demoted once on evidence; the honest sequence is to reopen that demotion first (see the next decision) rather than promote it by default.
  Q[Product & definition] Should we reopen the decision to demote authorship marking, given that it is now the only one of our four claims nobody has shipped?
     why: The corpus demoted authorship marking to a feature after finding it shipped-and-unsold in iA Writer 7. But between July and September 2026 every OTHER claim got absorbed by a platform, and attribution did not: the only implementation found is a VS Code extension with 88 installs.      rec: Reopen it in the narrower form. iA Writer 7's authorship marking was about pasted-versus-typed text in a personal document; attributing which spans of a repo document an agent wrote across sessions is a different product in a different context, and the iA Writer evidence does not actually cover it.   Q[Market & competition] Does Zed Delta go in the competitive set, and does it change what we build?
     why: Delta entered private beta on 2026-08-12 with the same thesis, the same vocabulary ("between commits", span-level attribution, comments that survive edits) and 679 HN points.      rec: Add it and reposition.   Q[Pricing & tiers] How do we price against a free markdown agent-review skill that got 1,241 GitHub stars in six weeks?

-- FINDINGS --
[opened|read-it] SPDX anchors a sub-file region with a REQUIRED byte range (cardinality 1..1) and an OPTIONAL line range (0..1) — and it settles the conflict in advance: byte range wins.
    ev: "the byte range values will take precedence" (SPDX 2.3 §9.4.1, on byte-vs-line disagreement)
    at: https://spdx.github.io/spdx-spec/v2.3/snippet-information/
[opened|read-it] SPDX gives its byte-range choice a stated rationale: byte ranges are format-independent, so they survive reformatting that would move line numbers.
    ev: "independent of various formatting concerns, and the most accurate way of referring to the differences"
    at: https://spdx.github.io/spdx-spec/v2.3/snippet-information/
[opened|read-it] An SPDX snippet is anchored twice: to a file identity (SnippetFromFileSPDXID, required) AND to the byte offsets within it. The offset alone is never the anchor.
    ev: "Uniquely identify the file in an SPDX document which this snippet is associated with" — Required Yes, 1..1
    at: https://spdx.github.io/spdx-spec/v2.3/snippet-information/
[opened|read-it] SPDX's package verification code is a SHA1 over the sorted SHA1s of every file, and it explicitly EXCLUDES the SPDX document itself so the manifest can live inside what it describes without changing the identifier.
    ev: "except the SPDX document itself, if it is included in the package"; "permits embedding the SPDX document within the package without altering the identifier"
    at: https://spdx.github.io/spdx-spec/v2.3/package-information/
[opened|read-it] SPDX anchors a file by relative path from the package root, required, exactly once — and the intent is stated as human findability, not identity.
    ev: "A relative filename with the root of the package archive or directory"; intent: "To aid finding the correct file"
    at: https://spdx.github.io/spdx-spec/v2.3/file-information/
[opened|read-it] C2PA splits anchoring into two named kinds: a HARD binding (cryptographic hash over exact bytes, proves both belonging and non-modification) and a SOFT binding (a fingerprint over content, which survives derivation but is not unique). A manifest may carry at most one hard binding and any number of soft bindings.
    ev: "A single manifest shall not contain more than one assertion defining a hard binding"
    at: https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.1/specs/C2PA_Specification.html
[opened|read-it] C2PA solves the self-reference problem the same way SPDX does: the hash covers the asset MINUS declared exclusion ranges, which may contain only the manifest and padding — and it warns that multiple exclusions open an insertion attack.
    ev: "it is desirable to have only a single exclusion range when possible"
    at: https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.1/specs/C2PA_Specification.html
[opened|read-it] C2PA distinguishes MISSING from STALE with two different status codes — `manifest.inaccessible` when the external manifest cannot be found or reached, and `assertion.dataHash.mismatch` when it is found but the bytes disagree. Neither outcome is a guess; the mismatch case is a hard rejection.
    ev: "the manifest.inaccessible error code shall be used to report the situation"; "the manifest shall be rejected with a failure code"
    at: https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.1/specs/C2PA_Specification.html
[opened|read-it] C2PA's own stated reason for choosing an external manifest over an embedded one is the frontmatter reason exactly: some assets must not be modified, and plain text cannot carry the manifest.
    ev: "It may not be appropriate, such as when it would modify an asset"; "not technically possible, such as with a .txt file"
    at: https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.1/specs/C2PA_Specification.html
[opened|read-it] When C2PA stores the manifest externally, it recommends writing a POINTER back into the asset's own metadata (a `dcterms:provenance` URI in embedded XMP), plus an HTTP `Link rel=c2pa-manifest` header. The sidecar is never left to be found by filename alone.
    ev: "add a dcterms:provenance key to the XMP, the value (a URI reference"
    at: https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.1/specs/C2PA_Specification.html
[opened|read-it] Adobe's XMP spec states the sidecar risk in one sentence, and its mitigation is a two-way anchor: write DocumentID/InstanceID INTO the described file so a format-aware app can confirm it has the right metadata.
    ev: "there is a risk that the metadata can be lost"; "place the values of the xmpMM:DocumentID, xmpMM:InstanceID ... within the file the XMP describes"
    at: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/adobe/XMP-Toolkit-SDK/main/docs/XMPSpecificationPart3.pdf
[opened|read-it] Adobe's sidecar-location rule is path-only: same directory, same basename, `.xmp` REPLACING the original extension. That is the entire discovery mechanism, and it is why a rename orphans the sidecar.
    ev: "the same name as the main document but with an .xmp extension"; MPEG-2: ".xmp replacing the original file extension"
    at: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/adobe/XMP-Toolkit-SDK/main/docs/XMPSpecificationPart3.pdf
[opened|read-it] XMP separates three identities that frontmatter currently conflates into one: DocumentID (stable across all versions and renditions), InstanceID (new on every save), and OriginalDocumentID (the ancestor across format conversions).
    ev: "xmpMM:InstanceID: Changes with each save operation"; "xmpMM:DocumentID: Created once for new resources"
    at: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/adobe/XMP-Toolkit-SDK/main/docs/XMPSpecificationPart1.pdf
[opened|read-it] XMP Annex A forbids fuzzy matching on identifiers outright: the only legal operations are create, assign, and compare — and comparison is a byte-for-byte equality check on the Unicode string. This is a refuse-rather-than-guess rule written into an ISO standard (ISO 16684-1).
    ev: "create them, to assign one to another, and to compare two of them for equality"; "a direct byte-for-byte check for equality"
    at: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/adobe/XMP-Toolkit-SDK/main/docs/XMPSpecificationPart1.pdf
[opened|read-it] Twenty years on, the photography world still has TWO incompatible sidecar naming conventions for the same format: Adobe replaces the extension (`IMG.xmp`), darktable appends it (`IMG.CR2.xmp`). darktable reads Adobe's form but will not write it, and generates a second sidecar of its own.
    ev: "Files with the latter naming format will be read by darktable but will not be written to"
    at: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/darktable-org/dtdocs/master/content/overview/sidecar-files/sidecar-import.md
[opened|read-it] darktable supports MULTIPLE sidecars per source file for parallel edit versions, disambiguated by an integer in the filename: `<basename>_<number>.<extension>.xmp`, with version zero at the unnumbered name.
    ev: "<basename>_<number>.<extension>.xmp, where <number> represents the version number of that edit"
    at: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/darktable-org/dtdocs/master/content/overview/sidecar-files/sidecar.md
[opened|read-it] darktable's default conflict resolution is silent clobber: once imported, the database wins and a foreign tool's edits to the sidecar are invisible and get overwritten. Detecting foreign edits and offering a choice is an OPT-IN startup scan, not the default.
    ev: "the database entries take precedence over the XMP file"; "such changes will be overwritten the next time darktable synchronizes"
    at: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/darktable-org/dtdocs/master/content/overview/sidecar-files/sidecar.md
[opened|read-it] darktable documents state that deliberately does NOT go in the sidecar (custom sort sequence numbers) and warns the omission is destructive on re-import — the sidecar is a partial projection of the database, and the gap bites on round-trip.
    ev: "This could be destructive if imported into another darktable image library"
    at: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/darktable-org/dtdocs/master/content/overview/sidecar-files/sidecar.md
[opened|read-it] Adobe's stated reason for sidecars in Lightroom Classic is byte-safety of the original — exactly frontmatter's splice-only, byte-exact premise, arrived at independently in a different medium.
    ev: "XMP metadata is stored in a separate sidecar file to avoid file corruption"
    at: https://helpx.adobe.com/lightroom-classic/help/metadata-basics-actions.html
[opened|read-it] The most-upvoted user critique of sidecars in photography is not that they are wrong but that they occupy an unstable middle: the same commenter reports XMP files are photographers' main complaint about darktable.
    ev: "They are easy to miss and hard to ignore." (HN comment 31660379)
    at: https://hn.algolia.com/api/v1/items/31660379
[opened|read-it] The rename failure is the canonical, textbook property of the whole sidecar class, not a darktable bug: the OS and file manager have no knowledge of the relationship.
    ev: "rename or move one of the files thereby breaking the relationship"
    at: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidecar_file
[opened|read-it] Git LFS pointers are canonicalised — exactly one valid byte encoding, sorted keys, <1024 bytes — AND require forward-compatibility: an old parser must round-trip keys it does not understand.
    ev: "there is exactly one valid encoding for a pointer file"; "MUST preserve keys that they don't know or care about"
    at: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/git-lfs/git-lfs/main/docs/spec.md
[opened|read-it] DVC anchors with BOTH path and content hash in the same record, and makes the path relative to the sidecar's own location by default (`wdir` defaults to the .dvc file's directory) — so moving the pair together does not break it.
    ev: outs entries carry `path` (Required) and `md5`; "wdir ... It defaults to `.` (the file's location)"
    at: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/iterative/dvc.org/main/content/docs/user-guide/project-structure/dvc-files.md
[opened|read-it] DVC resolves file-vs-sidecar disagreement with two explicit user verbs and no automatic winner: `dvc checkout` restores the file from the recorded hash, `dvc commit` accepts the file and rewrites the hash.
    ev: "forces DVC to accept any changed contents of tracked data currently in the workspace"
    at: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/iterative/dvc.org/main/content/docs/command-reference/commit.md
[opened|read-it] DVC's merge answer is a custom git merge driver registered through `.gitattributes` (`*.dvc merge=dvc`) that unions both branches — and it REFUSES rather than guessing when the same entry diverged on both sides.

-- IMPLICATIONS --
  * PRODUCT-BRIEF.md:135 says spans are anchored by "byte range + hash" and relocated by the splice locator. That is two of the three anchors the mature formats use. The missing one is a DOCUMENT IDENTITY that is independent of the path — and every format that survived twenty years has one. SPDX has SnippetFromFileSPDXID. C2PA writes a dcterms:provenance URI into the asset. XMP explicitly tells you to write xmpMM:DocumentID INTO the file the sidecar describes. frontmatter has the rare luxury that its documents ARE writable text with a conventional metadata block — the product is literally named after it. A `fm-id:` in YAML front matter is the anchor that survives rename, move, and copy, and it is the one thing the current design does not have.
  * A three-level anchor with a stated precedence order is the pattern, not one anchor: (1) document id read from the file's own front matter — authoritative, byte-for-byte equality only, never fuzzy; (2) repo-relative path — a hint for locating candidates, never identity; (3) span content hash + byte range — proves the span, with the byte range as tiebreaker exactly as SPDX §9.4.1 rules. Write down which one wins on disagreement BEFORE building, because SPDX, C2PA and DVC all did and darktable did not.
  * Byte offsets are not portable across a team as currently specified. Git normalizes text files to LF in the index and converts back to CRLF on Windows checkout by default. Every recorded offset shifts by one byte per preceding line for a Windows collaborator. The fix is one line — `*.md -text` or `*.md text eol=lf` in a shipped `.gitattributes` — and it must ship with the engine, not be assumed. `working-tree-encoding` makes it worse and should be refused outright.
  * The merge rule that decisions/questions.js:9390 records as missing has a proven shape. Make review.jsonl append-only, one immutable record per line, each carrying a stable record id; register `.frontmatter/*.jsonl merge=union` in a committed `.gitattributes`. `union` is BUILT IN to git, so it works on a fresh clone — a custom driver would not, because its definition lives in .git/config and does not travel with the repo. Union produces unordered lines; that is harmless if the reader sorts by timestamp and last-write-wins per (span, person). Blank lines are invalid JSONL, so a botched merge is detectable rather than silent.
  * Split the sidecar the way DVC split .dvc from dvc.lock. Authored facts (a person marked this span reviewed, at this time) are irreplaceable and must be merged. Derived facts (current relocated offsets, current staleness) are regenerable and should be deleted-and-recomputed on conflict, never hand-merged. Today's single review.jsonl mixes both.
  * Two distinct disagreement states, not one, following C2PA's manifest.inaccessible vs assertion.dataHash.mismatch: MISSING (the record names a document that is not there — do not guess which renamed file it is; offer the candidate set and let a person confirm) and STALE (the document is there but its bytes moved — the splice locator may relocate the span, and if it cannot, the record degrades to unreviewed rather than being deleted). Deleting an unmatched record silently is the failure that makes a review sidecar untrustworthy.
  * Never delete a record you cannot match. XMP's own spec says the risk of a sidecar is that the metadata is lost; darktable's default of silently overwriting foreign edits is the documented behaviour that photographers complain about most. Retain-and-mark beats prune.
  * Schema versioning: put a `v` on every line and translate on READ, per Cambria's central finding. Never rewrite the whole sidecar to migrate it — the file is in a git repo, so two branches will hold two schema versions simultaneously and a write-time migration turns that into a merge conflict on every line. Follow Git LFS: a parser MUST round-trip keys it does not recognise, so an older frontmatter does not silently strip a newer one's fields.
  * The sidecar must exclude itself from anything it hashes and must never be an input to review state, following SPDX's package-verification-code exclusion and C2PA's exclusion ranges. Otherwise reading the sidecar changes the sidecar.
  * Ship `.frontmatter/*.jsonl linguist-generated` in `.gitattributes` so the sidecar collapses in GitHub PR diffs and does not appear in language stats. A review-state product whose own churn makes code review worse will be deleted by the first reviewer who notices.
  * The naming conflict in the corpus (review.jsonl in the plan vs provenance.jsonl in the prototype) is not cosmetic — it is the darktable/Lightroom split in miniature, twenty years compressed into one repo. Two names means two readers means one of them read-only. Settle it before the first byte is written.
  * Extended attributes are ruled out as a carrier, measured on this machine: a custom xattr survives cp, mv and tar on APFS but is destroyed by the write-temp-then-rename atomic save that most editors perform, and git does not store it at all. The sidecar-as-file decision is correct; this is the evidence for it.
  * The single strongest counter-evidence to the sidecar approach, from the photographers who have lived with it longest: sidecars are 'easy to miss and hard to ignore'. Design for the miss — the engine must behave correctly when the sidecar is absent, gitignored by a collaborator's global ignore file, or dropped by an export — and it must never be the case that losing review.jsonl damages the markdown.

-- DECISIONS THIS FORCES --
  Q[Engine & technical] Should frontmatter write its own id into the document's YAML front matter, so review records survive a rename?
     why: Every long-lived sidecar format that solved the rename problem solved it by putting an identifier inside the described file — XMP tells you to write xmpMM:DocumentID into the file, C2PA writes a dcterms:provenance URI, SPDX carries a SnippetFromFileSPDXID. The formats that could NOT do this (raw camera files, which are unwritable) are exactly the ones still losing metadata on rename after twenty years. frontmatter's documents ARE writable text with a conventional metadata block, so it has an option photography never had. But writing an id in means the product that promises byte-exactness modifies the user's file, which cuts against the headline claim, and my own measurement shows git reports no rename at all when an agent renames and rewrites in the same commit — which is the common agent case.
     options: Write an `fm-id` key into each document's YAML front matter on first review; anchor records to it; fall back to path when absent | Never touch the document; anchor to path + content hash only, and treat a rename as a missing document requiring human confirmation | Write the id only when the user opts in per-repo, and degrade to path+hash otherwise | Anchor to a whole-document content hash instead of an id, so a pure rename with no edit relocates automatically
     rec: Opt-in per repo (option c), defaulting ON for repos where documents already carry front matter and OFF where they do not. It is the only option that both keeps the byte-exact promise honest for people who care and gives rename survival to the majority who will not notice a two-line metadata key. Whichever is chosen, the fallback path must be REFUSE-and-offer-candidates, never a similarity guess — XMP's ISO text is explicit that the only legal identifier operation is byte-for-byte equality.
  Q[Engine & technical] What wins when the sidecar and the document disagree — and is there ever an automatic winner?
     why: darktable made the database win silently, and that default is the single thing photographers complain about most; DVC refused to pick and shipped two explicit verbs (`dvc checkout` restores from the record, `dvc commit` accepts the file); C2PA does not resolve at all, it rejects with a named failure code and separates 'cannot find it' from 'found it and it disagrees'. frontmatter's plan does not state a rule. The product's whole claim is that it shows you review state you can trust, so a silent wrong answer here is worse than no answer.
     options: Document always wins; the record relocates via the splice locator or degrades to unreviewed | Record always wins; the span is shown as reviewed at the last known offsets | No automatic winner — two explicit user actions, DVC-style, plus a named state for each kind of disagreement | Relocate silently when the splice locator is confident above a threshold, prompt otherwise
     rec: Option c, with C2PA's two-code split made visible: MISSING (document not found — never guess which renamed file it is) and STALE (found, bytes moved). Relocation may run automatically only when the span's content hash matches exactly somewhere in the new bytes; anything short of an exact match degrades the record to unreviewed and says so. A threshold-based silent relocation (option d) is the darktable failure with extra steps — it produces a green 'reviewed' badge on text nobody read, which is the one output this product must never emit.
  Q[Engine & technical] How does `.frontmatter/review.jsonl` merge when two branches touch it?
     why: decisions/questions.js:9390 records that no merge rule exists anywhere in the plan, the gap register or the week-0 decisions. Sync is MVP-2 but the merge happens at MVP-0, the moment two people on one repo both use it. Git's built-in `union` driver is the only merge behaviour that works on a fresh clone — a custom driver's definition lives in .git/config and does not travel with the repository, so `.gitattributes` alone silently does nothing. DVC chose a custom driver and accepted that setup cost; it also made the driver FAIL rather than guess when the same entry diverged.
     options: Append-only JSONL + built-in `merge=union` in a committed `.gitattributes`; reader sorts by timestamp, last-write-wins per (span, person) | A custom frontmatter merge driver that reconciles semantically, with a documented one-time `git config` step per clone | Leave it to the default text merge and show conflict markers in a frontmatter conflict view | Do not commit the sidecar at all; keep review state local per machine
     rec: Option a. Union merge is the reason to use JSONL rather than JSON in the first place, and it needs zero per-clone setup. Its documented weakness — unordered output — costs nothing for an append-only log read through a fold. Make every line immutable and carry a record id so 'unreview' is a new line, never an edit to an old one; a JSONL file where lines are mutated is not union-mergeable and the format choice stops paying for itself.
  Q[Engine & technical] Do byte offsets get pinned with a shipped `.gitattributes`, or does the engine stop recording raw offsets?
     why: Git normalizes text files to LF in the index and converts back to CRLF on checkout, with CRLF the default on Windows. A byte range recorded on a Mac is off by one byte per preceding line on a Windows collaborator's identical checkout of the identical commit. This is invisible until the first Windows user joins, and it makes every span silently point one line too early — a splice engine that refuses rather than guesses will then refuse on everything, which reads as the product being broken.
     options: Ship `*.md text eol=lf` (or `-text`) in a `.gitattributes` the engine writes on init, and record raw byte offsets | Record offsets against a normalized-LF view of the file and convert at read time on CRLF working trees | Anchor spans by content hash plus a line/character position rather than a byte offset | Both: normalize via .gitattributes AND store an LF-normalized offset so the file is correct either way
     rec: Option d. The .gitattributes line is free and prevents the problem for repos frontmatter initializes; the LF-normalized internal offset makes the engine correct in repos it does not control, which is most of them. Also refuse outright on any path carrying `working-tree-encoding` — git will re-encode the whole file (UTF-16 for .ps1 is the documented example) and no byte offset survives that.
  Q[Engine & technical] Is review.jsonl one file, or two — authored facts and derived state?
     why: DVC separates .dvc (authored, must be merged) from dvc.lock (derived, safe to delete and regenerate), and that split is what makes its conflicts tractable — the docs say outright there is no need to resolve lock conflicts manually. frontmatter's single review.jsonl currently mixes an irreplaceable human fact (a person read this span at this time) with regenerable machinery (the span's current offsets, current staleness). Merging the regenerable half is wasted risk; merging the authored half is mandatory.
     options: One file, all of it merged | Two files: an append-only authored journal that merges, and a derived index that is gitignored and rebuilt | Two files, both committed, with the derived one marked linguist-generated and regenerated on conflict | One file, but every line tagged authored|derived, with derived lines dropped on merge
     rec: Option b. Gitignoring the derived index removes an entire class of merge conflict and an entire class of PR-diff noise, and it makes the honest claim testable: delete the derived file, rebuild it, and the review state must be identical. Whatever is committed should still carry `linguist-generated` so it collapses in GitHub diffs.
  Q[Name & identity] Is the sidecar named review.jsonl or provenance.jsonl — and does one file carry both review state and authorship?
     why: The plan (PRODUCT-BRIEF.md:135) says review.jsonl; the prototype says provenance.jsonl; decisions/questions.js:10254 already flags the split. This is not cosmetic. The darktable/Lightroom story is exactly what two names produce: one tool reads the other's file but refuses to write it, and generates a second sidecar of its own, forever. Whatever ships first becomes the compatibility surface for every later reader.
     options: One file, `review.jsonl`, carrying review state and authorship rows in the same journal | One file, `provenance.jsonl`, framing the product as provenance with review as one row type | Two files with distinct lifetimes: review state (per-person, mutable-by-append) and provenance (per-span, write-once) | Defer, and let the week-1 interviews name it
     rec: Pick one name this week and write it into the brief, the prototype and the spec in the same commit. On the substance, option a: a single journal with a `kind` discriminator on each line is simpler to union-merge than two files with different conflict semantics, and the product's own claim — see what the agent changed that you did not ask for, and what nobody has read — is one story, not two. Do not defer; the cost of the split is already visible in the corpus.
  Q[Engine & technical] How does the sidecar's schema version, and does an older frontmatter break a newer one's file?
     why: Cambria's central finding after months of production use is that schema translation in decentralised data must run on read, not on write — write-time migration cannot cope with schemas registered later. A sidecar in a git repo is decentralised by construction: two branches will hold two schema versions at once, and Cambria noted that schema history is non-linear even inside one company. Git LFS solved the same problem with a one-line rule: a parser must round-trip keys it does not understand.
     options: Version each line with a `v` field; translate on read; never rewrite the file to migrate; require unknown-key preservation | Version the file as a whole and migrate the entire sidecar on first open by a newer version | No versioning; add fields additively and treat unknown fields as ignorable | Versioned lines plus a documented lens/translation layer, Cambria-style, for non-trivial shape changes
     rec: Option a now, option d only if a field ever needs to change shape rather than be added. Per-line versioning plus mandatory unknown-key round-tripping is the cheap version of Cambria's result and it is what Git LFS actually shipped. A whole-file migration on open (option b) turns every schema bump into a merge conflict on every line of the sidecar across every open branch.
  Q[Flow & interaction] What does the UI show when a record cannot be matched to any document — and is 'unreviewed' the right degradation?
     why: This is the moment the product's promise is either kept or quietly broken. C2PA gives the disagreement two distinct names and rejects rather than resolving; darktable resolves silently and that is its most-complained-about behaviour. frontmatter's claim is about knowing what nobody has read, so the dangerous direction is asymmetric: showing 'reviewed' on unread text destroys the product, while showing 'unreviewed' on read text merely annoys.
     options: Degrade every unmatchable record to unreviewed, retain the record, and surface a 'N records could not be matched' affordance with candidate documents | Delete unmatchable records silently and rebuild from scratch | Keep the record's last known state and mark the document as 'stale review state' | Block the editor until the user resolves every unmatched record
     rec: Option a. Retain-and-mark, never prune — XMP's spec names loss as the sidecar's characteristic risk, and a review journal that quietly forgets is worse than none. Offering the candidate set (documents whose content hash matches the recorded span) turns a rename into a one-click confirmation rather than a guess, which is the same posture as the splice engine itself. Blocking (option d) makes the first agent-driven mass rename feel like the product broke.

-- IMPLICATIONS --
  * Do not put a note-taking market size in any deck, plan, or page. Six firms give 2024–25 figures spanning $1.35B to $15.46B, and one firm (TBRC) publishes two figures 5.0x apart for the same year under two report titles. Quoting any of them is quoting a number the seller made up. If a TAM slide is required, build it bottom-up from things that were actually counted: 14.47M VS Code installs of one markdown extension, Obsidian's 10,000+ organizations, Mintlify's claimed 20,000+ companies — and label each as vendor-reported.
  * The market is barbelled with nothing in the middle. Individuals pay $15–50 once, or $30–48 a year. Docs-platform budgets pay $3,000–5,400 a year. There is no observed price point between roughly $50/yr and roughly $2,000/yr in this category. A team tier at $4–5/user/mo (the current pilot hypothesis, per the memory file) sits in the empty middle and will have to justify why the gap exists rather than assume it is unserved.
  * Almanac is the single most important precedent and it must be read before the founder meeting. It was 'GitHub for Docs', it raised $34M, it shipped Read Receipts (know what nobody has read), Version Control Mode, Approvals and Doc History — the whole review-state thesis — and it shut down on 2025-01-31. The stated cause was not that nobody wanted it; it was that a faster-growing AI product ate the team's capacity. For a two-person studio the lesson is inverted and encouraging: the failure mode was divided attention, not absent demand.
  * Dendron is the shape-match and the warning: markdown, local-first, developers, inside the IDE, YC W21, 7,465 stars, 168,830 installs — and it died because it could not find PMF 'for a venture backed business'. That qualifier is the whole finding. Nothing in the record says it could not have been a good two-person business; what it could not be was a venture outcome. frontmatter should state explicitly, in writing, which of those it is trying to be, because the two require opposite decisions about pricing, scope and speed.
  * Obsidian is the honest ceiling and the honest floor. Eight people, no investors, category leadership, users at Amazon and Google — and it monetises only sync and publishing, never the editor, never the seat, and in Feb 2025 it voluntarily made its $50/user/yr commercial licence optional. If frontmatter intends to charge per seat for an editor, it is doing the thing the most successful independent in this exact category examined and rejected.
  * 'Confluence revenue' does not exist as a public number. Atlassian's FY26 10-K discloses revenue in exactly two categories, subscription and other. Any competitive slide that sizes Confluence is either citing an estimate farm or inventing it. What IS citable and audited: Atlassian did $6.572B in FY26, ~95% subscription, growing 26% — which sizes the ceiling of the whole team-wiki category without pretending to isolate one product.
  * The docs-as-code vendors have already established that this buyer tolerates high, unbundled prices — ReadMe sells AI as a $150/mo add-on on top of $250/mo; GitBook charges per site AND per user; Mintlify Pro is $450/mo. If frontmatter's review-state claim lands with the docs-platform buyer rather than the individual writer, the price is 50–100x higher than the prosumer instinct suggests. That is the most consequential open question the pricing decision has.
  * Notion's 100M users is a self-reported registered-account number from Sept 2024 and its revenue is genuinely undisclosed — the circulating $400M/$500M/$600M ARR figures come from three aggregators that contradict each other. Treat Notion as an unmeasurable comparator: use its $10/member/month price as the anchor, never its revenue.
  * Evernote's fate (most US staff cut, July 2023, post-acquisition) and Almanac's (shut down, Jan 2025) mean the two clearest recent outcomes in this category are harvest and abandonment. Neither was a competitor beating them on features. The durable survivors — Obsidian, Typora, iA Writer, Bear, Ulysses — are all small, all independent, and all sell either a one-time licence or a sub-$50/yr subscription.

-- DECISIONS THIS FORCES --
  Q[Pricing & tiers] Which price rung is frontmatter on — the $40-a-year one that indie markdown editors actually live at, or the $3,000-a-year one that docs platforms charge for the same file format?
     why: The opened pricing pages show a barbell with an empty middle: Obsidian Sync $48/yr, Bear $29.99/yr, Ulysses $39.99/yr, Typora $14.99 once, iA Writer $49.99 once — against ReadMe Pro $3,000/yr, GitBook Ultimate ~$3,708/yr for one site and five users, Mintlify Pro $5,400/yr. Mintlify Pro is 112x Obsidian Sync. Nothing observed sits between them. The current $4–5/user/mo team hypothesis lands squarely in the gap, so it needs an argument for why the gap is opportunity rather than a boundary the whole category has already found.
     options: Prosumer rung: one-time or sub-$50/yr, sold to the individual developer, sync/publish as the only recurring line | Docs-platform rung: $200-450/mo per workspace sold to whoever owns the documentation budget, priced against ReadMe and Mintlify | Deliberately occupy the empty middle at $4-5/user/mo and treat proving the middle exists as the pilot's primary hypothesis | Free editor forever, charge only for the review-state service (the Obsidian pattern: never charge for the editor, never charge per seat)
     rec: Free editor, paid review-state service, priced per repository or per workspace rather than per seat. This is the only option supported on both ends of the evidence: it copies what the one durable independent in this category actually does (Obsidian charges for sync and publish, never the editor, and gave up its per-seat commercial licence in Feb 2025), while pricing the paid part against the docs-platform buyer who demonstrably pays $250-450/mo. Per-seat in the middle is the one option with no observed precedent.
  Q[Business & operations] Is frontmatter trying to be a venture-scale business or a two-person business that pays two people well?
     why: Dendron's founder gave the cause of death in one sentence: not able to find product market fit 'for a venture backed business'. Everything else about Dendron looked healthy — 7,465 stars, 244 contributors, 168,830 installs, a live community. Almanac raised $34M and shut down; Obsidian took nothing and is eight people with users at Amazon and Google. The record says this category kills venture-shaped attempts and sustains small independent ones. That is a founding decision, not a later one, because it determines pricing, scope, hiring and how much of the market has to be captured for the thing to count as working.
     options: Explicitly non-venture: target a revenue number two people can live on, optimise for a decade of existence, never raise | Venture-shaped: chase the docs-platform budget, price at $200-450/mo, aim for the Mintlify trajectory and accept the failure rate | Deliberately undecided: build the pilot, see which buyer bites, decide at the meeting after this one | Open-source the engine, commercialise the review-state service — hedge by making the core outlive the company either way
     rec: Explicitly non-venture, stated in writing before the founder meeting. The evidence does not say the demand is absent — Almanac died of divided attention and Dendron of a funding shape, not of an empty market — it says the venture return is absent. Naming this now converts Dendron's failure from a warning into a non-applicable case, and it makes the Obsidian playbook (eight people, no investors, category leadership) the reference model rather than an aspiration.
  Q[Product & definition] Almanac shipped read receipts and shut down. Does 'know what nobody has read' stay the headline, or does it become a feature underneath the agent-diff claim?
     why: Almanac's still-live marketing page lists Read Receipts, Version Control Mode, Approvals, Layers and Doc History — the review-state thesis, built and sold to remote-work teams, with $34M behind it, dead as of 2025-01-31. This is the strongest available evidence that the read-tracking half of the pitch has been tried at scale. The distinguishing fact is that Almanac tracked human reads of human documents; frontmatter tracks human reads of AGENT changes, which did not exist as a problem in 2021. Whether that difference is load-bearing or cosmetic decides the headline.
     options: Keep 'what nobody has read' as the headline and differentiate on the agent-authorship angle explicitly | Demote it: lead with 'what the agent changed that you did not ask for', and treat read-state as supporting evidence | Lead with the splice engine's refusal behaviour — byte-exactness — and treat both review-state claims as consequences | Test both headlines in the pilot before the founder meeting and let the response decide
     rec: Demote read-state and lead with the agent-diff. Almanac proves the read-receipt framing can be built, funded, marketed and still not hold a team's attention; nothing in the record shows it was the thing customers came for. The agent-authorship framing is the part that did not exist when Almanac was built, so it is the part not yet disproven — and it is honest, because the working tree with no pull request is a genuinely new artifact.
  Q[Research & evidence] Do we ever cite a market-size number for note-taking or docs, in any deck, page or plan?
     why: The corpus was going to size this once. The finding is that it cannot be sized from these sources: one firm publishes $11.02B and $2.2B for the same base year under two report names, another says $1.35B for the same named market and year, and the six-firm spread is 11.4x. Two of the summary sentences are internally incoherent. Citing any of them puts a fabricated number into a document that is otherwise carefully sourced, and it is the kind of number a founder will repeat.
     options: Never cite a top-down market size; state the disagreement and move on | Cite the range explicitly as evidence that the category is unmeasured, and use it as an argument for bottom-up thinking | Build one bottom-up estimate from counted things (VS Code installs, Obsidian's 10,000 orgs, Mintlify's claimed 20,000 companies) and label every input's provenance | Commission or buy one real report
     rec: Never cite a top-down figure; build one bottom-up estimate with every input labelled as counted, vendor-claimed, or self-reported. The 8.2x same-year, same-market disagreement between TBRC and Dataintelo is itself the more useful line in a deck than any of the numbers it disagrees about — it tells a founder the category is too young or too diffuse to have been measured, which is a real fact about the opportunity.
  Q[Market & competition] Who is the buyer — the developer who opens the file, or whoever already pays ReadMe, GitBook or Mintlify for the docs?
     why: These are different products, not different segments of one. The developer buys at $15-50 and buys alone; the docs-budget owner buys at $250-450/mo, tolerates unbundled add-ons (ReadMe charges $150/mo extra for AI, $20 per extra admin) and buys per site. Everything downstream diverges: onboarding, whether SSO matters, whether the pilot needs multi-user at all, and whether review state is a personal reassurance or a compliance artifact.
     options: Individual developer working in a repo — single-player, no accounts, no server | Docs-budget owner — multi-seat, SSO, priced per repository, sold against Mintlify and ReadMe | Engineering manager reviewing agent output — the buyer neither of the above currently serves | Single-player first, and only add the team surface once one individual has paid twice
     rec: Single-player developer first, with the docs-budget buyer as the deliberate second act. Almanac and Dendron both died with a team-collaboration product; nothing observed shows a small team successfully selling a multi-seat docs tool. The single-player path also matches what the durable survivors sell and what two people can support. Design the price so the second act does not require repricing the first — per-repository, not per-seat.
  Q[Plan, scope & sequencing] What is the scope ceiling two people commit to, given that the category's most successful independent needs eight?
     why: Obsidian runs on eight named people and an office cat, with no investors, and that is the market leader. Almanac's shutdown letter names capacity as the cause: a small team could not maintain two products at quality. frontmatter's plan currently spans a splice engine, a review-state surface, rendering lanes, sync, and a documented multi-part roadmap. The two clearest failures in this lens were both capacity failures, not demand failures.
     options: Ship only the splice engine plus the agent-diff view; defer sync, publishing, teams and rendering indefinitely | Match the Obsidian shape: editor plus exactly one paid service, nothing else, for the first two years | Keep the full roadmap but sequence it behind a single revenue milestone that must be hit before anything is added | Reduce to a VS Code extension or CLI and skip the standalone app entirely
     rec: Editor plus exactly one paid service, and write down the list of things that are explicitly NOT being built. Almanac's letter is the argument: it did not fail on the market, it failed on attention, and it said so. A two-person studio with a multi-lane roadmap is running Almanac's experiment with a quarter of the people. The scope list belongs in the founder meeting as a decision, not in the plan as an assumption.

-- IMPLICATIONS --
  * Kill voice/dictation outright, and say why in one line if anyone asks. Apple gave every app free on-device speech-to-text in macOS/iOS 26 (SpeechAnalyzer/SpeechTranscriber); macOS Dictation already types into any text field with auto-punctuation and "new paragraph"; 25+ free Mac dictation apps shipped in 18 months and the two most-upvoted are open source; the paid survivors ($8–15/mo) are system-wide layers that will type into frontmatter without our doing anything. Building it means competing with a horizontal commodity inside our own text area.
  * Do not build a template gallery, a template marketplace, or a snippet library. Snippets specifically are dead (#332, 49k in Obsidian). The template money that exists is Notion database money at $6–49 a unit — the value is the schema, relations and rollups, none of which survive as plain markdown.
  * But do NOT conclude templates are unwanted. Templater is #2 of 7,403 with the highest latest-version install count in the entire ecosystem. Read what it actually is: a scripting engine for creating notes with variables and prompts, not a library of documents. The demand is programmable note creation, not curated content.
  * The agent-native form of that demand is a prompt, not a file. If frontmatter does anything here, it is: repo-local skeletons the agent fills, where the reviewable artifact is still the diff. That is one small feature inside the existing thesis, not a new product line. Ship it late, cheap, and without a gallery.
  * URGENT — Notion shipped our headline claim on 2026-08-28: agents propose changes instead of applying them, and you approve each one top to bottom. Our differentiation can no longer be "you can review agent edits." It has to be the two things Notion structurally cannot do: it works on a git working tree with no PR, and it is byte-exact on files other tools own.
  * The strongest validation of the thesis came out of this lens by accident. In Obsidian: agent-writing plugins at 2.04M cumulative downloads (Claudian #13, Agent Client #92) against Version History Diff at #324/51,806 and the only review-state-shaped plugin at #1,270/6,961. Roughly 40:1 write-to-review. Use the ratio, but state the caveat honestly — Claudian's latest version has only 5,134 downloads, so cumulative overstates the active base.
  * Obsidian's CEO independently stated our premise about a different artifact class: automated plugin review exists because "coding agents accelerate the creation of plugins" and the queue hit 2,300. That is a quotable, dated, first-party statement that agent-authored volume breaks human review.
  * Obsidian core ships zero AI — no mention of AI, agent, MCP, Claude, diff or review across 20 releases in seven weeks. The largest markdown editor has left review state entirely to plugins. That is the opening, and it is also the warning: if it were easy and obvious, a 7,425-plugin ecosystem would have filled it.
  * Distribution question worth reopening: Obsidian now ships a CLI whose stated purpose is "give agentic tools the ability to interact with your vault," and it has just formalised PAID plugins with a developer dashboard and pricing. A paid frontmatter plugin is now a legitimate channel that did not exist before May 2026.
  * Feature-list gaps that are now genuine 2026 table stakes, not nice-to-haves: (a) database views over YAML frontmatter properties — Obsidian Bases is CORE, with table/cards/list/kanban and a .base syntax embeddable in markdown code blocks; (b) BYOK for models — Craft, 2026-06-05; (c) MCP and ACP agent connectivity; (d) RTL layout and system text-size scaling, both shipped by Obsidian in September 2026; high contrast mode, Notion 2026-07-30.
  * Engine hazard for the splice-only claim: Obsidian 1.14.0 invented a colour-highlight syntax where a leading emoji inside == == carries the colour, and .base code blocks now embed a query DSL inside markdown. Both are byte sequences our locator must not misread or normalise. Add them to the corpus red-proofing.
  * One naming note: an Obsidian plugin called "Frontmatter Tag Suggest" (#486, 28,537 downloads) already exists. Not a conflict, but the word is taken in this ecosystem and means the YAML block, which is either useful or confusing depending on positioning.

-- DECISIONS THIS FORCES --
  Q[Features] Do we ship voice dictation at all?
     why: It was on the candidate list, and it is the cheapest thing on that list to say no to — but only if we say no deliberately and write down the reason, so it does not come back every quarter. Apple made on-device speech-to-text free to every app in macOS/iOS 26; twenty-five free Mac dictation apps shipped in eighteen months; the paid survivors charge $8–15/mo for a system-wide layer that already types into any text field, ours included. Inside the biggest markdown ecosystem, dictation gets 1/99th the installs templates do, and more people install text-to-speech to hear their notes than install speech-to-text to write them.
     options: No, and document the reason so it stops resurfacing | No for now, revisit only if a paying customer asks unprompted | Yes, a thin wrapper over Apple's SpeechTranscriber (free, on-device) | Yes, full dictation with markdown-structure awareness (headings, lists by voice)
     rec: No, and document the reason. This is the clearest negative in the whole lens: the capability is free at the OS layer, the category is commoditised to zero, and one dictation startup (Ito) abandoned the product within a year of launching it. If a user wants to dictate into frontmatter today, they already can — Apple's Dictation and Wispr Flow both type into our editor without our writing a line. The only version with any argument behind it is voice that emits correct markdown structure, and that is a hard NLP problem paying for a demand signal we cannot find.
  Q[Features] Do we ship templates, and in which of the two very different meanings of the word?
     why: The corpus barely touched this, and the data is genuinely split. Templater is the #2 plugin in Obsidian and the most-installed by active-version count — that is real, large demand. But it is a scripting engine, not a scaffold library. Meanwhile snippets are dead (#332), iA Writer's "templates" are print styles, and the $6–49 template economy is Notion database templates whose value cannot exist in plain markdown. Getting this wrong in either direction is expensive: build a marketplace and we build a dead mall, build nothing and we miss the second-most-wanted capability in the category.
     options: Nothing — the agent generates the scaffold, so the template is now the prompt | Repo-local .md skeletons with a few variables, no gallery, no marketplace | A curated template library shipped with the product | A template marketplace with paid third-party templates
     rec: Repo-local skeletons with a few variables, and only after the review-state core is solid. Frame it as "new document from a file in this repo," not as a feature called Templates — the word means print styles to iA Writer users and database apps to Notion users. Explicitly rule out the marketplace: Obsidian's brand-new commerce-enabled directory has no template category at all, three independent Notion template marketplaces are already dead or domain-squatted, and the one enormous success in markdown templating is AGPL donationware.
  Q[Product & definition] Notion now lets an agent propose edits you approve one at a time. What is our headline claim now?
     why: On 2026-08-28 Notion shipped, in its own words, agents that propose changes instead of making them directly, with per-change approval top to bottom. That is our stated headline. Every Notion release for the two months before it was agent infrastructure. If our pitch stays "see what the agent changed before it lands," we are describing a feature the largest competitor gives away, and the meeting deck is out of date.
     options: Keep the claim, differentiate on the working tree with no PR | Keep the claim, differentiate on byte-exactness over files we do not own | Narrow to the git-native case explicitly: the diff nobody opened a PR for | Broaden to review state across a whole repo, not one document
     rec: Narrow to the git-native case and say the sentence out loud: this is the diff nobody opened a PR for. Notion can approve edits inside Notion's own storage; it structurally cannot show you what an agent did to a working tree on your disk that has no pull request, and it cannot be byte-exact about files that a build, a linter, and three other tools also write. Those two constraints are the moat and they should be the first line of the deck, not the third.
  Q[Features] Do we need database views over YAML frontmatter properties?
     why: Obsidian Bases is now a CORE plugin — table, cards, list and, as of 2026-09-02, kanban views over note properties, described by a .base file or a code block embedded in markdown. A product literally named frontmatter, shipping in 2026 with no way to query or view frontmatter properties across a repo, will be asked about this in the first demo. It also creates an engine obligation: .base blocks are a query DSL living inside markdown that our splice engine must not misread.
     options: No — out of scope, we are a review tool not a database | Read-only: a filterable table of documents by frontmatter property | Full Bases-equivalent with saved views | Defer, but make sure the engine handles .base blocks byte-exactly now
     rec: Read-only table now, and handle the .base block in the engine regardless. A filterable view of every document by frontmatter property is a natural fit for review state — filter by reviewed/unreviewed, by owner, by last-agent-touched — so this is not scope creep, it is the same question asked across a repo instead of one file. Do not build saved views or formulas; that is Obsidian's product, not ours.
  Q[Go-to-market & channels] Should frontmatter ship as an Obsidian plugin as well as a standalone editor?
     why: The channel changed in May 2026 and we have not re-evaluated since. Obsidian formalised paid plugins with pricing in a developer dashboard, launched a CLI whose stated purpose is letting agentic tools work on the vault, and hosts a community where the Claude-Code-in-your-vault plugin is #13 all-time while core ships zero review tooling — the nearest diff plugin is #324. That is a distribution surface with a demonstrated agent-editing population, a demonstrated absence of our category, and, for the first time, a way to charge.
     options: Standalone editor only | Standalone plus a free Obsidian plugin as a funnel | Standalone plus a PAID Obsidian plugin | Obsidian plugin first, standalone later
     rec: Standalone remains the product; treat a paid Obsidian plugin as a serious channel experiment rather than a distraction, but only after the engine is stable. The caution is real: 7,425 plugins have not filled this gap, which is either an opening or a signal that vault users tolerate unreviewed agent edits. The cheap way to find out which is to ship the plugin and watch whether anyone pays.
  Q[Engine & technical] Which byte-exactness hazards from the last six months go into the corpus now?
     why: The engine's promise is that it locates a byte range, replaces only those bytes, and refuses rather than guess. Two new syntaxes landed in the wild since the corpus was built: Obsidian 1.14.0's colour highlights, where a leading emoji inside == == carries meaning, and .base blocks, a query DSL embedded in markdown code fences. Both are byte sequences a normalising or over-clever locator can silently corrupt, and both now exist in real vaults.
     options: Add both to the corpus red-proofing immediately | Add colour highlights only | Treat both as out-of-spec and refuse on them | Do nothing until a user reports corruption
     rec: Add both to the corpus now. They cost a fixture each and they are exactly the class of thing that makes a byte-exactness claim false in the field rather than in the test suite. Refusing on them is also acceptable and arguably more honest, but refusing on a syntax that ships in the most popular markdown editor's default install will read as a bug.
  Q[Design, UI & attention] Are RTL layout, system text scaling and high contrast in scope for v1?
     why: Both major competitors shipped accessibility work in the last six weeks: Notion added high contrast mode on 2026-07-30, and Obsidian flipped its entire workspace for right-to-left languages and made text scale with the system's preferred size including larger accessibility sizes. This has moved from differentiator to expected. It is also much cheaper to design in than to retrofit, which makes it a v1 question rather than a later one.
     options: Full support in v1 — RTL, system text scaling, high contrast | System text scaling and contrast in v1, RTL later | None in v1, all as fast-follow | Contrast only
     rec: System text scaling and contrast in v1; RTL later but designed for. Text scaling and contrast are near-free if the design system respects them from the start and painful to add afterwards. Full RTL is a genuine layout project and our first users are English-language repo owners — but do not hardcode direction anywhere, because Obsidian just spent a release doing that retrofit in public and it was clearly expensive.

-- IMPLICATIONS --
  * frontmatter is NOT entering the redlining category and should stop implying it is. The redlining category's product is format reconstruction — OCR, embedded-Excel extraction, DOCX-to-PDF redline export, N-way version matching. In a markdown repo all of that is zero-value, because the bytes are already the same format and git already computes the changes. If frontmatter's pitch is 'a better diff for prose', it is selling the one thing developers have had free since 2006 (git --color-words, 10 Aug 2006).
  * The 11 mentions in 1.24M words is not a gap in the corpus — it is the correct weight. This lens produced almost no competitive threat and one very large negative result. The right output is a paragraph in the plan saying why this category is not a competitor, not a competitive-analysis section.
  * The category's price ceiling is real and knowable: Draftable $129/$261 per user/year, Litera Compare (Windows-only, <10 seats self-serve), Diffchecker $15/$20/$40 per user/month. frontmatter's team tier hypothesis of $4-5 sits an order of magnitude below the segment that actually pays for 'show me what changed'. That segment pays 3-5x more, and it pays because a missed change is a malpractice event, not because the diff is prettier.
  * Every one of these tools shows the diff in the same three shapes — side-by-side with synchronised scrolling, an inline redline, and a clickable change list. Litera uses three synchronised panes (original / modified / redline). If frontmatter's review-state UI does not have a change list you can click through and exhaust, it is missing the one affordance the whole 20-year-old category converged on. The change list, not the side-by-side, is what makes review state completable.
  * Granularity: nobody in this category sells below word level as a user-facing concept. Word's API offers exactly char-level and word-level, word being the default. Diffchecker sells word-level and character-level as two separate line items. frontmatter's splice-only byte-range engine is genuinely below the floor of this entire category — that is a real differentiator, but it is an engine property, not something any of these vendors has taught buyers to want. It has to be sold as a consequence (it refuses rather than guesses) not as a spec.
  * The dead-product record is unambiguous and all four deaths are in frontmatter's exact segment, not the legal one. Editorially (markdown + version system, 11 people, closed 30 May 2014, 'failed to attract enough users to be sustainable'), Poetica (five people, acquired by Condé Nast 1 Mar 2016, service dead 1 Jun 2016), Penflip ('GitHub, but for writing', ~$8/month, 502 by 2022), Draft (503 by 2025). The tools that live are the ones that sold to lawyers. The tools that died are the ones that sold to writers and developers. frontmatter is currently pointed at the second group.
  * Poetica's own words are the sharpest warning available: they sold their tool as an escape from 'arcane tools like git'. frontmatter sells the opposite — a tool that lives inside git. That is a genuinely new position, and it means there is no incumbent to displace, but it also means there is no demonstrated willingness to pay to inherit. The category's revenue exists because Word documents are opaque; frontmatter's substrate is transparent by construction.
  * There is one live, sourced, unexploited seam: nobody in this category has ever shipped a git integration. 'git' appears 0 times on Draftable's pricing and API pages. Litera's integration list is six DMS products and Outlook. Diffchecker lists a Git integration and a CLI, and its CLI got 98 npm downloads last month against `diff`'s 550 million. The seam is real and it is also demonstrably worth almost nothing to sell into — which is exactly why frontmatter's claim must be review state (who has read what, what the agent changed unasked), not diffing.
  * The buyer is the finding. Litera reaches 99% of the Am Law 100 and grew $16M to ~$250M; Workshare had 2M+ professionals; Litera Compare processed 8M+ documents in October 2023 alone. The money in 'show me what changed in this prose' is entirely in one vertical where the cost of a missed change is measured in liability. frontmatter's equivalent question is: for whom is an unreviewed agent edit a liability event, not an annoyance? That question, not the diff, is the product.
  * Windows-only is the category's tell and its permanent ceiling. Draftable still has no Mac client in 2026 ('Mac support - coming late 2026'); Litera Compare needs a VM or dual boot on macOS or Linux. Developers are disproportionately on macOS and Linux. This is not a strategic choice the vendors made against developers — it is evidence they never once considered them a market, over 27 years (Workshare, 1999) and 31 (Litera, 1995).

-- DECISIONS THIS FORCES --
  Q[Market & competition] Should the plan say plainly that document comparison and redlining tools are not competitors, and say why?
     why: The corpus mentions this category 11 times in 1.24 million words and never resolves whether it is a threat. Left unresolved, someone in a meeting will ask 'what about Litera' and there will be no answer. The research produced a clean, sourced negative: these tools sell format reconstruction (OCR, embedded Excel, DOCX-to-PDF redline), and none of that work exists when the corpus is plaintext in a repo.
     options: One paragraph in the plan stating the category is adjacent, not competitive, with the format-reconstruction reason and the Litera/Draftable numbers as evidence | A full competitive-analysis section treating them as competitors | Leave it unaddressed as it is now
     rec: One paragraph. Name Litera ($16M to ~$250M, 99% of Am Law 100) and Draftable ($129/$261 per user/year, Windows-only) so the reader sees the category is large and profitable, then state the reason it does not reach a markdown repo: their moat is bridging file formats, and in a repo there is only one format. This converts an open question into a settled one at a cost of about 120 words.
  Q[Flow & interaction] Does frontmatter ship a clickable, exhaustible change list, or only a side-by-side view?
     why: Every tool in this 20-year-old category converged on the same three surfaces — side-by-side with synchronised scrolling, an inline redline, and a change list you can click through. Litera runs three synchronised panes. The change list is the only one of the three that makes review completable: it tells you when you are done. Review state as a product claim is unbackable without it.
     options: Change list is the primary surface; side-by-side is a detail view opened from a list row | Side-by-side is primary with a change list as a secondary panel | Side-by-side only, with review state expressed as per-file read/unread marks
     rec: Change list primary. frontmatter's claim is 'what nobody has read', which is a counting claim — it needs an enumerable, markable list of changes, not a canvas. The side-by-side is where you go to adjudicate one row. Every profitable tool in this category made the same call, and it is the cheapest place to inherit their 20 years of UI learning.
  Q[Pricing & tiers] Is the team tier priced at $4-5, or is it repriced against what this category actually charges?
     why: The tools that solved 'show me what changed in this prose' and survived charge $129-$261 per user per year (Draftable) or $180-$480 per user per year (Diffchecker Pro/Legal/Enterprise). The tools that charged ~$8/month to writers and developers are all dead — Penflip, Draft, Editorially, Poetica, every one of them, between 2014 and 2025. The $4-5 hypothesis sits below the price point that has a 100% mortality rate in this exact segment.
     options: Hold $4-5 and accept it is a volume bet in a segment with no surviving volume precedent | Reprice to $15-25/user/month against Diffchecker Pro and Draftable Business | Two tiers: a free or near-free individual tier, and a team tier priced against the compliance buyer, not the writer buyer
     rec: Two tiers, and pick the team price by naming the buyer first. The sourced pattern is unambiguous: writer-priced prose tools died and lawyer-priced ones grew to $250M. Before setting a number, answer for whom an unreviewed agent edit is a liability rather than an annoyance — regulated docs, published API references, contracts-as-code, compliance runbooks. Price the team tier at that buyer, not at the individual writer.
  Q[Product & definition] Should the splice engine's byte-range granularity be surfaced to users at all, or only its consequence?
     why: No product in this category sells below word level as a user-facing concept. Word's API exposes exactly two granularities, character and word, defaulting to word. Diffchecker sells word-level and character-level as separate line items. Twenty years of buyers have never been taught to want byte ranges. Leading with the engine spec risks selling a number no one can evaluate.
     options: Lead with the engine: byte-exact, splice-only, character-precise | Lead with the consequence: it refuses rather than guesses, and never rewrites a byte you did not change | Both, with the consequence as headline and the engine as the proof underneath
     rec: Consequence first, engine as proof. 'It refuses rather than guess' is legible to anyone who has watched an agent silently reformat a file. 'Byte-range splice' is legible only to people who already believe you. The engine spec belongs in the technical section as the reason the consequence is credible, not on the front page.
  Q[Product & definition] Do we claim the git-native seam explicitly, given that it is empty and that emptiness is measured?
     why: No redlining vendor has ever shipped a git integration — 'git' appears zero times on Draftable's pricing and API pages, and Litera's integration list is six document management systems and Outlook. But the one vendor that did ship a CLI, Diffchecker, got 98 npm downloads last month against the plain `diff` package's 550 million. The seam is genuinely unoccupied and also demonstrably worth close to nothing as a diffing product.
     options: Claim the seam as positioning: the first comparison tool that lives inside the repo | Do not claim it; treat git-nativeness as an implementation detail and lead entirely on review state | Claim it narrowly: git-native is why review state is possible, not a feature in itself
     rec: Claim it narrowly. Git-nativeness is the enabling condition for knowing what an agent changed with no PR and who has read it — that is the sellable claim. Sold as 'diffing inside git' it is competing with a free tool that has 550 million monthly downloads. Sold as 'the review state git cannot compute for you', it is competing with nothing.
  Q[Research & evidence] Does the plan carry the dead-product record explicitly, with dates and stated causes?
     why: Four products in frontmatter's exact segment died between 2014 and 2025 — Editorially (closed 30 May 2014, 'failed to attract enough users to be sustainable'), Poetica (dead 1 Jun 2016), Penflip, Draft. All four sold prose collaboration to writers and developers. Every survivor in the category sold to lawyers. That is the single most decision-relevant fact this lens produced, and a founder meeting that does not contain it is a meeting where the graveyard is invisible.
     options: A short 'who tried this and died' section with the four names, dates and stated causes | A single line acknowledging prior attempts failed | Omit; it reads as arguing against the product
     rec: Include it as its own short section. It is not an argument against the product — it is the argument for choosing the buyer deliberately rather than by default. Poetica's own line about escaping 'arcane tools like git' is worth quoting verbatim, because frontmatter is the first entrant to take the opposite bet, and a bet nobody has taken is a very different risk from a bet four teams already lost.

-- IMPLICATIONS --
  * The repeated failure mode has a name and the people who lived it said it in almost the same words twice: the tool is not the business. Editorially — 'Even if all of our users paid up, it wouldn't be enough.' Poetica — building the humane editor and building the business model were 'often at odds ... competing for our limited time.' Both are statements about a small team burning its attention on a product whose addressable revenue can never fund the team maintaining it. Frontmatter's engine sophistication (splice-only, refuse-rather-than-guess) is on the axis that has never killed anything in this category, and its team size is on the axis that has killed everything.
  * The second repeated mode is the exit shape: four of eight (Editorially->Vox, Poetica->Condé Nast, Authorea->Atypon/Wiley, Manuscripts->Atypon) were bought as TEAMS by organisations that already owned a publishing pipeline, and in Poetica's case the acquirer explicitly took the software and refused the users. Editorial review state is worth buying to put inside an org with existing revenue; it has never been worth selling to the people doing the writing. If frontmatter is to be sold rather than absorbed, it must be sold to the org that carries the liability for unreviewed content, not to the writer who resents reviewing it.
  * Almanac is the single most important data point and it cuts both ways. It shipped frontmatter's headline feature set — Version Control Mode, Layers, Approvals, Read Receipts ('ensure your team actually reads important knowledge'), scheduled deprecation reviews, two-point doc history — reached 7-figure ARR on $45M and 50+ people, and shut down 2025-01-31. But its stated reason was NOT that review state failed to sell; it was that the founders' AI content product grew faster and consumed the team. That is the difference between 'this proposition is dead' and 'this proposition was abandoned', and frontmatter should not let anyone collapse the two.
  * The most uncomfortable Almanac detail: the only thing that ever had organic pull was the templates gallery — tens of thousands of monthly users during COVID — and the founder deliberately killed it to chase 'GitHub for docs'. In this category the workflow is what the founders want to build and the content is what users show up for. Frontmatter should assume its own equivalent exists and watch for it, rather than killing it.
  * Every product that showed git to writers got the same review, verbatim on HN in 2014: 'It's far too technical looking. It's too much like Git.' Poetica made its entire pitch out of escaping 'arcane tools like git'. Overleaf, the category's one commercial survivor, wins because academics 'don't want to deal with git workflows'. Frontmatter's escape from this is not a better git UI — it is that its users are ALREADY in repositories and already carry the git model, which makes its audience structurally different from every corpse in this lens. That distinction is the pitch, and it should be stated as such rather than assumed.
  * GitBook is the survival template and the warning: it kept the brand and killed the substrate. The git-native CLI carries a deprecation notice, legacy.gitbook.com now redirects with utm_campaign=close_legacy, and git survives only as a bi-directional Git Sync integration into GitBook's own editor. The market rewarded owning the editing surface and treating the repo as a peripheral. Frontmatter's byte-exact repo-native position is the opposite bet and needs a reason why 2026 is different from 2018.
  * Two of eight died silently — Penflip went 200 to 502 in a month in mid-2020 with its last blog post 15 months after launch, and Draft has served 'Offline for Maintenance' continuously since April 2023 while generating not one HN comment. There is no community in this space loud enough to notice a death. Frontmatter should not plan on organic discovery, HN, or word-of-mouth; the graveyard proves the audience is too diffuse to make noise even when a beloved tool disappears.
  * There is a live 2026 entrant, docs.dev, in exactly the adjacent slot — and its founders reached this problem by first KILLING their own standalone 'AI writes your docs' product, on the reasoning that Claude Code and Cursor's agents made it unnecessary. Their answer to agent-written docs is 'review the rendered page as readers see it', not 'review the bytes'. That is a direct, testable positioning collision with frontmatter's byte-exact review state, and it is the only competitor found in this lens that is alive and post-agent.
  * Editorially's arithmetic is the pricing constraint, stated by a funded team with 7 people: full conversion of the users they had would not have paid for the engineers needed to maintain the app. Penflip's $8/mo GitHub-clone pricing was called out as unfundable on the day it was announced. Any frontmatter pricing that anchors to a GitHub-style per-seat figure is repeating a mistake that was diagnosed publicly in 2014 and confirmed by two deaths since.
  * Absorption is the modal outcome, not the failure case. If frontmatter's realistic best exit is being bought by a company that owns a documentation pipeline (a docs platform, a CMS, an agent vendor), then the product should be built to be legible to that buyer — a review-state layer that plugs into an existing pipeline — rather than as a standalone editor competing for the writer's daily habit. That is a plan, not a concession.

-- DECISIONS THIS FORCES --
  Q[Pricing & tiers] Who is the person who pays — the writer working in the repo, or the organisation that has to answer for unreviewed agent-written content?
     why: Four of the eight products traced were absorbed as teams by organisations that already owned a publishing pipeline, and Condé Nast explicitly bought Poetica's software while refusing its users. Editorially's own goodbye says full conversion of individual users could not fund a maintained app. The graveyard says individual writers do not fund this category; organisations with a content obligation do. Frontmatter's current framing ('what nobody has read') is legible to both and priced for neither.
     options: Sell to the individual technical writer / developer at a self-serve price, accept the Editorially arithmetic, and keep the team small enough that it works | Sell to the team or org that owns the docs repo, on the accountability argument: agent commits with no PR are an unreviewed-change liability | Sell to a platform (docs host, CMS, agent vendor) as an embedded review-state layer, treating absorption as the plan rather than the failure | Free/open individual tier as distribution, paid org tier as revenue — the GitBook shape
     rec: Price and pitch to the org that carries the liability, with a free individual tier purely as distribution. The individual-writer market has now killed six products with public death certificates; no product in this lens ever died from an org refusing to pay for review state, because almost none of them ever asked an org.
  Q[Product & definition] Does frontmatter lead with review state, or with something people already show up for?
     why: Almanac reached 7-figure ARR and $45M and shipped the full review-state kit — Version Control Mode, Layers, Approvals, Read Receipts, deprecation reviews — and shut down. Its founder's own account says the one thing with organic pull was a templates gallery he killed to chase the workflow vision. Every dead product in this lens led with authoring or workflow; none of them led with review state, so there is no direct precedent either way, but there is strong precedent that the workflow claim is what founders want and not what users arrive for.
     options: Lead with review state ('see what the agent changed that you did not ask for') and treat editing as support | Lead with the byte-exact editor and surface review state as the reason it is trustworthy | Lead with a narrow, urgent job (audit an agent's working tree before you commit) and let review state be the mechanism, not the headline | Run both headlines against real traffic before committing
     rec: Lead with the narrow urgent job, not the capability. 'Review state' is a category word; 'the agent touched 41 files and you have read 6 of them' is a moment. Almanac's arc is the argument for not naming your product after the abstraction you personally find interesting.
  Q[Flow & interaction] Is git the product's visible surface, or its invisible plumbing?
     why: Every product that exposed git to writers collected the same complaint — Penflip got 'too technical looking, too much like Git' from its own users on HN. Poetica built its entire pitch on escaping 'arcane tools like git'. GitBook survived by demoting git from substrate to a sync integration and retiring the git-native CLI. Overleaf, the one commercial survivor, wins because users do not want git workflows. Frontmatter's audience is genuinely different — people already in repos — but that difference is an assumption the graveyard has never tested.
     options: Fully git-native and visible: commits, diffs, branches, refs are the interface | Git as substrate, review state as interface — the repo is real but the user never types a git command | GitBook's shape: own the editing surface, sync to the repo bi-directionally | Repo-native for developers, a hidden mode for non-developer reviewers on the same tree
     rec: Git as substrate, review state as interface. Frontmatter's byte-exact splice engine is a correctness guarantee, not a user-facing feature, and the only correct place to show a commit hash is when something went wrong.
  Q[Market & competition] Should frontmatter position against docs.dev's 'review the rendered page' model, or accept that as a different market?
     why: docs.dev launched in July 2026 into exactly this gap, having first killed its own AI-doc-generation product on the reasoning that Claude Code and Cursor made it unnecessary. Its stated premise — 'reviewing agent-written docs in raw MDX is miserable' — is the same premise as frontmatter's, with the opposite answer: review the rendered page as readers see it, publish via git commit. This is the only live post-agent competitor found in this lens and the collision is on the core claim.
     options: Position directly against it: byte-exact source review beats rendered-page review because the bytes are what the next agent reads | Accept the split: they own docs-sites, frontmatter owns working-tree review for any markdown repo | Absorb the idea: offer both a rendered and a byte view of the same splice | Ignore it until it has traction (it launched at 2 points on HN)
     rec: Accept the split publicly and absorb the idea quietly. Their premise validates frontmatter's; their answer serves a published docs site, frontmatter's serves an uncommitted working tree. But a rendered view of a splice is cheap and removes the only objection their pitch makes well.
  Q[Plan, scope & sequencing] How much engine investment is justified before there is a paying user?
     why: Not one product in this graveyard died because its version model, diff or merge was wrong. Every stated cause of death is market size, business model, founder capacity, or acquisition. Poetica named the exact trap: for a five-person team, the humane-engineering goal and the business-model goal 'were often at odds ... competing for our limited time'. Frontmatter has already invested heavily in splice-only correctness and refusal semantics, which is the axis that has never killed anything here and is on the axis (attention) that killed Poetica.
     options: Freeze engine work at current correctness and spend everything on finding the buyer | Keep hardening the engine because refusal correctness is the defensible moat | Timebox: a fixed number of weeks on distribution before any further engine work | Split by evidence: only engine work that a named prospective user has blocked on
     rec: Split by evidence. Refusal correctness is genuinely a moat, but Poetica's sentence is the closest thing this lens has to a direct warning addressed to frontmatter, and it was written by five smart people who lost to exactly this trade.
  Q[Business & operations] Is being acquired by a docs platform, CMS or agent vendor a stated goal or a fallback?
     why: Absorption is the modal outcome in this category, not the exception: Editorially to Vox Media's product team, Poetica's technology to Condé Nast's Copilot, Authorea and Manuscripts both into Atypon/Wiley. Products built to be standalone editors were bought for their teams and their tech and then discontinued. A product built from the start to be an embeddable review-state layer would be bought for itself.
     options: Build standalone, treat acquisition as an outcome you do not design for | Build standalone but keep the review-state core cleanly extractable and documented as an integration surface | Build integration-first: ship as a layer over an existing docs pipeline before shipping a standalone app | Do not plan for it at all; a repo-native local tool has no natural acquirer
     rec: Standalone product, extractable core. The four absorbed products all had their tech bought and their users discarded; the difference between a $2M acqui-hire and a real acquisition is whether the valuable part can be lifted out cleanly.
  Q[Go-to-market & channels] How does frontmatter reach anyone, given that this category cannot generate noise even when a loved product dies?
     why: Penflip went from live to 502 in a month in mid-2020 and nobody wrote about it. Draft has served 'Offline for Maintenance' since April 2023 and produced zero HN comments in seven years, despite being the tool HN recommended most when Editorially died. Almanac's 'GitHub for Docs' Show HN got 6 points and no comments. The category has no watching community, so organic discovery is not a channel here.
     options: Direct outbound to teams whose repos visibly carry agent commits with no PR | Distribution through the agent ecosystem — ship as a Claude Code / Cursor adjunct where the users already are | Content and demo-led, accepting slow compounding | Open-source core for developer distribution, paid org features for revenue
     rec: Distribution through the agent ecosystem. The audience frontmatter needs is not 'writers' — it is people already running agents against repositories, and that is the one population in 2026 that does congregate and does talk.

-- IMPLICATIONS --
  * Law is not an adjacent market for frontmatter and should not be treated as one. The substrate is .docx, the editor is Microsoft Word, the delivery mechanism is an email attachment, and the 2026 AI drafting winners (Spellbook, GenieAI) ship as Word ribbon add-ins. Every markdown-for-law project I could open is either dead (legal-markdown, archived 2014, 123 stars) or tiny after seven years (Accord Project markdown-transform, 86 stars). Nothing about frontmatter's format bet reaches this buyer.
  * There IS a real adjacent buyer one door over, and it is not lawyers: medical-device and regulated-software teams already keeping their QMS, IEC 62304 and 510(k) documentation as .md in git and reviewing it in pull requests. Innolitics RDM proved documents produced that way pass a 510(k) submission and an IEC 62304 audit. It is dormant (last push 2022-10-27) and OpenRegulatory's competing template set is markdown too. That segment has frontmatter's exact substrate, an unmet review problem, and a budget line called compliance.
  * S1000D is the design document frontmatter should read before shipping review state. Aerospace formalised this decades ago: change markup on individual elements typed add/modify/delete, each linked by reasonForUpdateRefIds to a reason record; quality assurance that resets to "unverified" the moment an official object is edited; an explicit override flag for edits that do not change content and therefore need no re-verification; separate first and second verification with a verification method; and the old issue file made read-only. That is a complete, battle-tested state machine for what frontmatter calls review state, and copying its semantics is free.
  * Every regulation I opened mandates document-level approval plus a non-obscuring change record. Not one mandates per-span review state. 21 CFR 211.186 wants two handwritten signatures on a whole master record; 21 CFR 211.100 wants written procedures including any changes approved by the QC unit; FAA Part 121.135 wants a revision date per PAGE. So per-span review state is a better product, not a legal requirement, and pitching it as a requirement would be false.
  * The compliance driver that IS real is narrower and sharper: the audit trail rule. 21 CFR 11.10(e), EU Annex 11 §12.4, ICH E6(R3) §2.12.2 and MHRA's guidance all say the same thing in four dialects — record who changed what, when and why, and never overwrite the prior value. frontmatter's splice-only, refuse-rather-than-guess engine is a literal implementation of that sentence. That is the sentence to put on the page, quoted, when talking to any regulated buyer.
  * MHRA has already adjudicated frontmatter's central UI question. It says audit trails may be reviewed either as a full list or by an exception-reporting process, and defines exception reporting as a validated search that surfaces predetermined abnormal actions needing a reviewer's attention. That is regulatory cover for showing the reviewer only what is unreviewed and surprising, rather than every change — the exact bet the product makes.
  * The strongest external endorsement of the product thesis is the EMA's September 2024 reflection paper: AI drafting or editing regulatory documents needs close human supervision and a quality review mechanism covering all model-generated text before submission. Being able to say which text a model wrote is the precondition for complying with that sentence. It is a "should" in a non-binding paper, so it is an anchor for a narrative and a landing page, not evidence of a purchase order.
  * "Compare against the last version I approved" is already shipped and named in this market — Draftable pitched "Changes Since Opened" in 2013 and still sells it. frontmatter does not get to claim that as a new idea; it gets to claim doing it per span, in the repo, against an agent's commit rather than a Word session.
  * Pricing reference points, all read off pages: Draftable charges $129/user/year for a pure diff tool and $261/user/year for its legal tier. GenieAI charges $75/month for one seat, $600/month for enterprise. Everything above that (Litera, Luminance, Ironclad, Kira) is quote-only and I could not open a price. A single-purpose comparison tool sustaining $129/seat/year for thirteen years is the most encouraging number in this lens for a narrow, well-made product.
  * Two direct ancestors died and both matter more than any live competitor. Draft (draftin.com) shipped exactly frontmatter's per-change accept/ignore on markdown with marked major versions; it is gone, last archived 2023-03-08. Editorially shipped collaborative markdown with versioning and closed five months after opening publicly, offline 30 May 2014. Neither died of a bad idea — both were general writing tools with no forced trigger. frontmatter's forced trigger is the agent that edits the tree while nobody is watching, which is the one thing neither of them had.
  * Atrium is the cautionary tale for the legal detour specifically: $75.5M and 100 people, shut down 3 March 2020, because it could not beat a traditional law firm on efficiency. The 2012 HN consensus on git-for-law reached the same verdict eight years earlier — the blocker was adoption, not architecture. If frontmatter spends a quarter on lawyers it will rediscover this.

-- DECISIONS THIS FORCES --
  Q[Market & competition] Do we go after regulated-software teams — medical device, IEC 62304, GxP — who already keep their SOPs as markdown in git?
     why: This is the only genuinely adjacent buyer the research found. They already have frontmatter's substrate (markdown in a repo), they already review in pull requests, they have a budget line called compliance, and the two open-source tools serving them are dormant or template-only. It is a different landing page, a different proof burden (audit-trail retention, export), and possibly a different tier — so it cannot be done accidentally.
     options: Ignore it; stay purely on engineering docs written by agents | Add one landing page and a compliance FAQ quoting 21 CFR 11.10(e) and Annex 11 §12.4, and see who arrives | Build the retained, exportable audit trail properly and sell a compliance tier | Partner with or fork OpenRegulatory's markdown ISO 13485 templates as a distribution wedge
     rec: Add the landing page and the quoted-regulation FAQ now; it costs a day and the quotes are already gathered. Do not build a compliance tier until someone from that world asks for an export, because the requirement is document-level approval plus a retained trail, which is a storage and export problem, not an editor problem.
  Q[Features] Should a span's review state reset to unreviewed the moment its bytes change, with an explicit override for edits that don't change meaning?
     why: S1000D has run exactly this rule in aerospace for decades: quality assurance is set back to unverified when an official object is edited, and there is a dedicated flag to say this edit does not affect contents and so does not require re-verification. Choosing the same semantics makes review state honest but noisy; choosing anything softer makes it a lie under audit. It also decides whether a whitespace or reflow edit nukes a page of green.
     options: Any byte change resets the span to unreviewed, no exceptions | Reset by default, with an explicit 'no content change' override the editor records | Reset only when the rendered text changes, not when formatting does | Keep review state on the span identity and let the reviewer decide
     rec: Reset by default with a recorded override, the S1000D model. The override must itself be a logged, attributed action, otherwise it is a hole in the trail.
  Q[Flow & interaction] Does approving a span record a reason, or just a name and a timestamp?
     why: Every regulation opened wants the why, not just the who and when — MHRA states the audit trail carries the who, what, when and why, and S1000D links each element's change mark to a reason-for-update record. A reason field costs friction on every approval and changes the review UI substantially, so it has to be decided before the interaction is built, not bolted on.
     options: Name plus timestamp only — keep approval one keystroke | Optional free-text reason | Required reason for rejections and overrides, optional for plain approvals | Structured reason types, the way S1000D types its updates
     rec: Required on rejections and on the 'no content change' override, optional on plain approvals. That gets the why where an auditor or a colleague actually needs it without taxing the common case.
  Q[Design, UI & attention] When a reviewer opens a file with forty agent-made changes, do we show them all, or only the ones that look like they need attention?
     why: This is the core attention bet and it is now externally adjudicated: MHRA explicitly permits reviewing an audit trail either as a full list or by exception reporting, defined as a validated search surfacing predetermined abnormal actions. Exception reporting is the more useful product and the harder one — it requires a defensible definition of 'abnormal' and it can hide something that mattered.
     options: Full list, always — the reviewer decides what matters | Exception-first with a one-click reveal of everything | Exception-only | Full list by default, exception view as a named mode
     rec: Exception-first with everything one click away, and make the rule that decides 'exception' visible and inspectable. A hidden heuristic is the thing that will lose a user's trust the first time it hides something real.
  Q[Legal, privacy & data] Do we quote regulators on the marketing pages, and if so are we honest that none of them require per-span review?
     why: The quotes are unusually good — the EMA says model-generated text needs a quality review mechanism, 21 CFR 11.10(e) says record changes shall not obscure previously recorded information. They will convert. But no regulation opened mandates span-level review state; pharma mandates whole-document approval and aviation mandates a per-page revision date. Implying a mandate that does not exist is the kind of claim that gets found out by exactly the buyer we would be courting.
     options: Do not quote regulators at all | Quote them and clearly frame frontmatter as exceeding the requirement, not satisfying it | Quote them without the caveat | Quote only the EMA AI paragraph, which is about AI-written text specifically
     rec: Quote them with the caveat stated plainly on the same page: the rule is document-level approval plus an audit trail that does not obscure prior values; frontmatter goes finer than the rule asks. Compliance people reward that sentence and punish its absence.
  Q[Product & definition] Is a stable 'last version I approved' baseline a first-class object in frontmatter, or is review just per-commit?
     why: Draftable has sold 'Changes Since Opened' since 2013 and it is the feature lawyers name. In a repo with an agent committing overnight, 'since my last approval' and 'since the last commit' are different questions and the first is the valuable one. Making the approval a durable named baseline changes the storage model and enables diffing across many intervening agent commits.
     options: Review is per-commit only | Store a per-reviewer, per-file approved baseline and diff against it | Store a per-span approved byte range, S1000D style | Both a file baseline and per-span state
     rec: Per-span approved state, with a derived per-file 'everything you have seen' baseline for the headline view. Per-span is what makes the product different; the file-level baseline is what makes the first screen legible.
  Q[Market & competition] Do we spend anything at all on lawyers as a buyer in the next two quarters?
     why: Lawyers pay well and redline all day, so the pull is real. But the substrate is .docx in Word, the AI incumbents are Word add-ins with thousands of firms already, Atrium burned $75.5M failing to change law-firm workflow, and every markdown-for-law project is dead or tiny. A quarter spent here is a quarter not spent on the buyer whose documents are already in a repo.
     options: No — treat law as out of scope and say so | Borrow the vocabulary and UI patterns only; sell to nobody in law | Ship a docx import path as an experiment | Pursue in-house legal teams at software companies, who already live near a repo
     rec: Borrow the patterns, sell to nobody in law. The redline/blackline vocabulary and the compare-to-approved-version idea are worth taking; the buyer is not. Revisit only if an in-house legal team at a design-partner software company asks unprompted.
  Q[Engine & technical] Do we adopt semantic line breaks — one sentence per line — as a recommended convention, and does the editor help with it?
     why: It is the known trick that makes prose diff cleanly in git, and it surfaced independently in the lawyer-adjacent Word-diffing discussion. For a splice-only engine it changes how often a change is a tight span versus a whole reflowed paragraph, which directly changes how good the review view looks. But it also rewrites files in ways that could look like the exact unrequested agent edit frontmatter exists to catch.
     options: Ignore it — never touch line breaks | Detect and respect an existing one-sentence-per-line file, but never impose it | Offer it as an explicit, opt-in reformat | Recommend it in docs and let the user's agent do it
     rec: Detect and respect, never impose. A tool whose pitch is that it does not change bytes you did not ask it to change cannot ship a reflow that rewrites every line of a document.
