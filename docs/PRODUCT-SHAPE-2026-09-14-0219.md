# What to sell, to whom, and at what price

You said you are confident on the product and stuck on how it fits together, how it sells to
both D2C and enterprise, and what the price categories are. This answers those three, and it
opens with a finding that changes one of them.

## 1. The uncomfortable finding

I went looking for who else turns an idea into something an agent can build from. The
category exists, it is a year old, and it is crowded at the top.

**GitHub's own Spec Kit.** Fetched 2026-09-14. "Define what to build before building it, with
any AI coding agent." Free, open source, **136,339 stars and 12,249 forks**. First commit
2025-08-21, last push 2026-09-12, so it was being worked on yesterday. It reached 1.0.0 last
month. The README describes it as "built for your whole organization".

**Kiro, from AWS.** "Turn prompts into executable specs." A desktop app and a CLI, with cloud
sessions. Tiers are Free, Pro, Pro Plus, Power and Enterprise, priced per user, with $20
appearing as the Pro figure and a $0.04 unit that reads like per-request overage. They are
running a student programme across 132 universities.

**Tessl** calls itself an "Agent Enablement Platform", which is the category name written
down. **Traycer** is in the same space.

So: the thing we were going to lead with, generating a document kit from a prompt, is a
category where **the free option comes from GitHub and has 136,000 stars, and the paid one
comes from Amazon.**

That does not kill the product. It kills one sentence, and we should kill it deliberately
rather than find out in month four.

## 2. What this actually changes

Generation is not a wedge. It is a feature, and it is one the two largest players in the
market give away or subsidise.

But look at what all four of them are. Spec Kit is a toolkit that writes files into your
repository and then you are back in VS Code. Kiro is an IDE, which means it is competing with
Cursor for the same window. Tessl is a platform. Traycer is a layer over your agent.

**None of them is the place a human reads, edits, approves and keeps current what the agent
was told.** They all generate. Generation is a single act. What happens afterwards is the
part nobody has taken, and it is the part that never ends.

The evidence for that being real work, from the sample on 13 September:

- `openai/codex` has made **77 commits** to its `AGENTS.md`
- `apache/airflow` has made 64, and its file is **36,577 bytes, roughly 6,100 words**
- `apache/airflow` and `vercel/next.js` both keep `CLAUDE.md` as a **git symlink** to
  `AGENTS.md`, because two copies is a maintenance problem they solved with a filesystem trick
- `danny-avila/LibreChat` kept two real files, and they have **drifted to 7,415 bytes against
  23,170**

Seventy-seven commits is not a file somebody generated. It is a document somebody maintains,
and the tool they maintain it in is a code editor with no idea what the file means.

**So the sentence is:**

> frontmatter is where you read, edit and approve the documents your agents obey.

Generation sits inside that. We will still write the first draft of your kit, because a blank
page is a bad start. We are not going to claim that as the difference, because GitHub gives
it away and Amazon subsidises it.

## 3. How D2C and B2B are one product

This was the part you said you could not put together. They are not two products. They are
the same file at two scales.

**One developer** has one `AGENTS.md`, a few thousand words, and a nagging feeling the agent
is ignoring half of it. They want to paste model output somewhere clean, get a PDF out, and
keep their instruction file from rotting.

**A team** has the monorepo problem. The main OpenAI repository holds **88 instruction
files**. Agents read the nearest one up the tree, so which one actually governs a given file
is a question nobody can answer by looking. Four engineers edit them independently. Nobody
reviews what an agent wrote into a spec. The whole fleet behaves inconsistently and no one can
say why.

**An organisation** has the scope above that, and it already exists: the Claude Code manual
documents a **managed policy file deployed by IT across every user in the organisation**. That
is a governance surface, shipped, with no tooling around it at all.

Same artefact. One file, then eighty-eight files, then a policy. The product grows with the
customer instead of switching.

That is the answer to "how do I sell to both". You do not. You sell one thing and the price
follows how many files and how many people.

## 4. What to sell, by tier

| Tier | Who | What they get | What they are paying for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Free** | anyone | The editor at `/` with no account. Paste, clean up, export to PDF, HTML and Word. Local only | Nothing. This is acquisition |
| **Pro** | one developer | Storage and sync, share a link, your own model key, the kit generator, templates | Where the files live |
| **Team** | a squad running agents | Instruction files across a repo, which file wins for a path, who changed what, review state on the files agents obey, size warnings | Knowing what the fleet was told |
| **Enterprise** | an organisation | Org-wide policy files, audit of every change, SSO, and a record of who approved what | Governance |

The free tier is not generosity. Section 3.3 of the feature research measured it: dillinger,
StackEdit and Excalidraw all show zero sign-in words on the landing page and put the editor
straight on screen. Our page today **is** the login screen. That is the funnel's missing top
and it is now card `AC20`.

The Team tier is the one that matters commercially, and it is the only tier whose problem
nobody else is solving.

## 5. Price categories

Anchored on what was measured, not on what feels right.

**What the market does.** Obsidian gives the editor away and charges for Sync and Publish,
with prices on the page including $8, $5, $4 and $50, and a separate commercial licence.
HackMD runs Free, Prime and Enterprise. Kiro runs Free, Pro, Pro Plus, Power and Enterprise,
per user, with $20 on the Pro row.

**What I would propose, as a starting position to be tested in the twenty conversations:**

| Tier | Price | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Free | nothing | Matches every tool a developer reaches for. Also the whole funnel |
| Pro | around $6 to $8 a month | Obsidian's Sync sits in this band and it is the closest comparable |
| Team | around $15 to $20 per seat | Kiro's Pro row is $20 per user, so this is the band the buyer already accepts |
| Enterprise | not priced on the page | Say "talk to us" until a real one asks. Pricing it early invents a number |

Two things I would not do. I would not price the editor, because nobody in this market
charges for one. And I would not put a number on Enterprise before somebody has asked for it,
because the number would be invented and then quoted back at us.

**One warning on ₹ and the Indian market.** ₹15,000 per transaction is an architectural
constant under RBI rules, and Indian cards get one payment attempt. Whatever the ladder ends
up being, the Indian pricing is a separate design problem and not a currency conversion.

## 6. The AIOS question

You mentioned AIOS and custom skills. I want to be careful here.

Everything in section 4 is one artefact at three scales, which is why it holds together. AIOS
is a different thing: an orchestration layer with its own surface, its own concepts and its
own buyer. Adding it to this ladder would make the product two products, and card `FF14`
already asks how many surfaces one person at 1.21 engineering days a week can carry. The
honest answer to that card is one.

Custom skills are different again, and they may genuinely belong. A skill is a markdown file
with instructions that an agent reads, which is the same artefact as everything else in
section 3. If skills are authored the way `AGENTS.md` is authored, they are a row in the Team
tier, not a new product.

I would put AIOS down and keep skills, and I would put that as a card rather than deciding it
here.

## 7. Should you answer the questions first?

Yes, and start tonight. Here is the reasoning rather than just the answer.

Four of the five gating cards are untouched by today's finding. `L1` is about whether we hold
bytes. `B1` is who builds and how fast. `PR24` is whether we charge for the editor or for
storage, and section 5 makes that one easier rather than harder. `P2` asks where review state
sits, and today's research makes the answer I recommended stronger, not weaker.

Only `FF1` moves, because "what are we building and on what surface" now has to account for a
free GitHub toolkit with 136,000 stars in the same space. I will update that card before you
reach it.

What I would not do is wait for a perfect product plan before answering. The five cards are
how the plan gets decided. This document is an input to them, not a replacement for them.

**Order for tonight:** `L1`, then `B1`, then `PR24`. Those three are pure and none of them
depends on anything I found today. Stop there. Take `FF1` and `P2` in the next sitting, after
you have both read section 2 and argued about it.

## 8. What is still unknown

The same gap as always, and it has not moved. **Nobody has been asked whether they would
pay.** Every price in section 5 is anchored on what other companies charge, which tells you
what a buyer has accepted elsewhere and not what they would give us. Twenty conversations
closes it and nothing else does.

I also have not verified how Kiro's tiers map to features, only that the tier names and the
$20 figure appear on their pricing page. And Spec Kit's 136,339 stars measure attention
rather than use, which are not the same thing, though at that scale the distinction matters
less than it usually would.
